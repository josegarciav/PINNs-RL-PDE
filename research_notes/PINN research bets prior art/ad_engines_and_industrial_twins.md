# AD engines for PINN derivatives (forward/Taylor mode, compiled kernels) and industrial deployment of PINN/hybrid digital twins

Research date: 2026-11-19. Evidence labels used below: **[PR]** peer-reviewed (journal or main-track conference), **[WS]** workshop paper, **[PP]** preprint, **[TH]** thesis, **[DOC]** software documentation or forum, **[REG]** regulator or standards body, **[VENDOR]** vendor or vendor-sponsored claim. Numbers are quoted with the setup the source gives; where I read the full text myself (PDF extraction) I say so, otherwise the number comes from an abstract or a search summary.

## (A1) Key papers on Taylor-mode / forward-mode AD for PINN derivatives, and the speed-ups they report over nested reverse mode

### Takeaway
Published speed-ups of exact forward/Taylor schemes over nested first-order AD are modest for second order (about 2x for the Laplacian, on GPU, with large MLPs) and grow with derivative order (about 2x at orders 3-7 in STDE's low-dimensional test, 3-3.6x for a fourth-order KS residual, 9x for a stochastic biharmonic, "over 7x" at five derivatives and a projected 25x+ at seven for n-TangentProp); the 62x and >1000x headline figures come from changing the problem (separable architecture, randomized estimation in very high dimension), not from the AD mode alone. Every benchmark found is on a GPU; none reports CPU step times.

### Cited Findings

Foundations
- Taylor-mode AD (propagating truncated Taylor coefficients, with efficient recurrences for elementary functions) is the classical technique of Griewank and Walther, *Evaluating Derivatives* (SIAM, 2nd ed. 2008), chapter 13; Dangel et al. cite it as "[13, §13]" and note that their own implementation does **not** use those efficient schemes but "stick[s] to Faà di Bruno" (full text read). **[PR]** — [Dangel et al., arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- Bettencourt, Johnson, Duvenaud, "Taylor-Mode Automatic Differentiation for Higher-Order Derivatives in JAX", NeurIPS 2019 Workshop on Program Transformations; this is the origin of `jax.experimental.jet`. **[WS]** — [ML Anthology record](https://mlanthology.org/neuripsw/2019/bettencourt2019neuripsw-taylormode)
- A JuliaCon poster from the same group states the scaling as nested first order O(exp D) versus Taylor mode O(D log D) in derivative order D (secondary source, not the workshop paper itself). — [JuliaCon poster](https://live.juliacon.org/uploads/posters/QDD7G7.pdf)
- JAX's Taylor mode is still described in 2025 as "experimental" by Dangel et al. (full text read). — [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)

Collapsing Taylor mode (Dangel, Siebert, Zeinhofer, Walther; NeurIPS 2025) **[PR]** — full text read
- Method: a graph rewrite (PyTorch `torch.fx`) that propagates the *sum* of the highest Taylor coefficients instead of each one; it generalises the forward Laplacian to arbitrary linear PDE operators and to randomized Taylor mode. The authors say the rewrite "could be done by a machine learning compiler". — [NeurIPS 2025 abstract](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/d835e25415cfe4d6d48a0be3b7b763bb-Abstract-Conference.html); [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- Setup: Nvidia RTX 6000 GPU (24 GiB); tanh MLP D → 768 → 768 → 512 → 512 → 1, D = 50 for the exact Laplacian; smallest of 50 repetitions; metric is the slope of time versus batch size (ms per datum). — [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- Vanilla Taylor mode loses to nesting: "evaluating the Laplacian of a 5-layer MLP, using JAX's Taylor mode is 50% slower than nested backpropagation" (JAX + jit, GPU). JAX table G5, Laplacian per datum: nested first-order 0.58 ms (1.0x), standard Taylor 0.84 ms (1.5x), collapsed/forward Laplacian 0.29 ms (0.50x). — [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- PyTorch: nested first-order 0.61 ms per datum; standard Taylor 0.56 ms; collapsed 0.29 ms (ratio ≈ 0.52, theory (2+D)/(1+2D) ≈ 0.51 for D = 50). — [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- Versus nested first-order AD: "for the exact and stochastic (weighted) Laplacians, collapsed Taylor mode is roughly twice as fast ... while using only 40-50% memory. For the biharmonic operator ... in the stochastic case up to 9x in time, and 5x in memory (differentiable)". — [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- Compilation finding: with `torch.compile`, "compiling can further reduce the memory footprint ... while the runtime remains roughly identical"; and "JAX's jit compiler is unable to apply the graph rewrites we propose". — [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- Stated limitations: only "a small number of primitives" supported, because Taylor arithmetic "needs to be implemented case by case"; not fully optimized (no in-place operations). — [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)

Stochastic Taylor Derivative Estimator (Shi, Hu, Lin, Kawaguchi; NeurIPS 2024, oral, best paper award) **[PR]** — full text read
- Abstract claim: ">1000× speed-up and >30× memory reduction over randomization with first-order AD", and 1-million-dimensional PDEs "in 8 minutes on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU". — [arXiv 2412.00088](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00088v2); [NeurIPS page](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/poster/95741)
- The >1000x is against the original PyTorch SDGD baseline. The paper decomposes it: re-implementing the baseline in JAX "provides ∼15× speed-up and up to ∼4× memory reduction"; parallelization another "∼15×"; and against "the best realization of baseline method SDGD, the parallelized stacked backward mode AD, STDE provides up to 10× speed up and memory reduction of at least 4×". — [arXiv 2412.00088](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.00088)
- Setup: single NVIDIA A100 40 GB, CUDA 12.2, JAX (`jet.jet`); 4-layer tanh MLP, 128 hidden units (1024 for some cases). — [arXiv 2412.00088](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.00088)
- Low-dimensional, high-order test (the regime closest to ordinary PINNs), Table 11, iterations/s, base network L = 4, h = 128: 2D KdV backward 762.86 vs STDE 1372.41; 2D KP 766.79 vs 1518.82; 1D gradient-enhanced KdV 621.04 vs 1307.27. With h = 1024: 349.23 vs 418.13 (KdV), 340.94 vs 642.21 (KP), 293.97 vs 441.50 (g-KdV). The text summarises this as "around ∼2× speed up compared to repeated application of backward mode AD across different network sizes". The g-KdV case uses "one 2-jet and two 7-jet pushforward". — [arXiv 2412.00088](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.00088)
- Forward Laplacian inside STDE's ablation: "the best method in both speed and memory when the dimension is 100", but being exact its cost is O(d), and "when the dimension is larger than 1000, it becomes worse than even parallelized stacked backward mode SDGD". — [arXiv 2412.00088](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.00088)
- Complexity statement: repeated backward mode costs O(2^(k-1)(d + (L-1)h)) memory, exponential in derivative order k. — [arXiv 2412.00088](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.00088)

Forward Laplacian (Li, Ye, Jiang, Wen, Wang, Li, Li, He, Chen, Ren, Wang; arXiv July 2023) **[PP as found]**
- Abstract: "achieves a remarkable speed-up by more than one order of magnitude" for neural-network variational Monte Carlo (the gain includes a new architecture, not only the Laplacian scheme). — [arXiv 2307.08214](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08214)
- Independent measurement: the JAX implementation (`folx`) "is roughly twice as fast as nested first-order AD" (Dangel et al., GPU, MLP above). — [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- A second independent comparison (KFAC for PINNs paper) reports the forward Laplacian roughly twice as fast as the functorch Laplacian, and about half the memory at D = 100 (as summarised by search; batch 1024, large MLP). — [arXiv 2405.15603](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.15603)

DOF (Li et al., ICLR 2024 workshop) **[WS]**
- Abstract: "2x improvement on the MLP structure and nearly 20x improvement on the MLP with Jacobian sparsity" over Hessian-based AutoDiff for second-order operators; "halving the computation cost and reducing memory usage by a third". — [arXiv 2402.09730](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.09730)

Zero Coordinate Shift (Leng, Shankar, Thiyagalingam; J. Comput. Phys. 505, 2024) **[PR]**
- A reverse-mode trick (one scalar leaf per coordinate dimension) for physics-informed operator learning; ">90% reductions in GPU memory and wall time" for PI-DeepONets; one figure about 3 GB (ZCS) versus about 78 GB. Implemented by extending DeepXDE (PyTorch backend; authors say TensorFlow and JAX behave similarly). — [arXiv 2311.00860](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.00860v3)

Separable PINNs (Cho, Nam, Yang, Yun, Hong, Park; NeurIPS 2023) **[PR]**
- Per-axis networks plus forward-mode AD: "62x in wall-clock time and 1,394x in FLOPs given the same number of collocation points" on multi-dimensional PDEs; a chaotic Navier-Stokes case in 9 minutes versus 10 hours for the best prior method, single GPU. The earlier workshop version reported 57x and 1,195x. — [NeurIPS 2023 proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/4af827e7d0b7bdae6097d44977e87534-Abstract.html); [arXiv 2306.15969](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15969); [workshop version](https://nips.cc/virtual/2022/59890)

n-TangentProp (Kyle R. Chickering, UC Davis; arXiv Dec 2024) **[PP]** — fetched HTML
- Exact forward computation of all n input derivatives in one pass; claimed quasilinear cost versus exponential for repeated autograd. — [arXiv 2412.09752](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09752v1)
- End-to-end PINN training on self-similar Burgers profiles: "over 2.5 times" faster with 3 derivatives; "over 7 times" with 5; with 7 derivatives n-TangentProp took "a little under 1 hour" against a *projected* "over 25 hours" for autodiff; with 9 derivatives, under 1.5 hours against an *estimated* "at least 100 hours". Network: 3 hidden layers of 24 neurons, batch 256, single NVIDIA A6000. — [arXiv 2412.09752](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09752v1)
- Caveats stated by the author: autodiff "outperformed" n-TangentProp on the backward pass for small derivative counts; gains shrink with batch size and width; pure-Python implementation; two of the four timings are projections. — [arXiv 2412.09752](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09752v1)

Residual-specific explicit derivative kernels (Cao, Lu, Zhang; arXiv 2606.29702, 29 June 2026) **[PP]** — full text read. **This is the closest prior art to a per-equation engine.**
- "Residual-specific partial-jet derivative propagation": only the derivative states the target PDE residual needs are propagated, through "specialized layerwise kernels, rather than relying solely on nested AD or a generic Taylor-mode transform". Kernels are written in PyTorch/JAX by a programming agent in a "generate–verify–revise loop" and checked against nested AD to floating-point level. — [arXiv 2606.29702](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29702)
- Scope limit that matters for novelty claims: "In all cases, parameter gradients for training are still computed by reverse-mode differentiation of the scalar loss; ED replaces only the coordinate-derivative evaluation inside the PDE residual." — [arXiv 2606.29702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29702)
- Setup: single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5080, float32, full-batch Adam, 1000 steps, mean of three runs after 20 warm-up steps; Burgers and 2D lid-driven Navier-Stokes with tanh MLP 6 × 64; Kuramoto-Sivashinsky (fourth order) with a modified MLP and Fourier embedding, 4096 collocation points. — [arXiv 2606.29702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29702)
- PyTorch end-to-end training speed-up over nested AD: 1.22× (Burgers), 2.30× (NS), 3.58× (KS). — [arXiv 2606.29702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29702)
- JAX/XLA, same benchmark: Taylor-mode AD (coordinate-direction jets) over nested AD 1.31×, 1.47×, 1.76×; explicit kernels over nested AD 2.28×, 2.27×, 3.01× (Burgers, NS, KS). — [arXiv 2606.29702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29702)
- Stated limitation: kernels are "specialized to the prescribed network architecture, activation function, residual form"; changes require them "to be regenerated and reverified". — [arXiv 2606.29702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29702)

Other 2026 preprints
- Schettini Gherardini, "A user's guide to PINNs in geometric analysis" (arXiv 2607.28733, 30 July 2026): replacing nested reverse mode by forward propagation of second-order jets and compiling the graph once "reduce the cost of a training step by roughly 40 to 50 times" on identical hardware (abstract). A search summary of the full text attributes about one order of magnitude to `torch.func` forward transforms over nested autograd and a further 2.9-4.9x to a compiled jet evaluator, and blames "per-operation overhead (Python dispatch, kernel launch, allocation of intermediates)"; I did not verify those two sub-figures in the PDF. **[PP]** — [arXiv 2607.28733](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28733)
- Mikulski and Uhl, "Derivative Computation in PINNs: Automatic Differentiation, Finite Differences and Beyond" (arXiv 2608.11020, Aug 2026): finite differences with calibrated step match AD accuracy on three benchmark PDEs, run faster across the tested batch sizes and use less GPU memory; no numbers in the abstract. **[PP]** — [arXiv 2608.11020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11020)

DeepXDE
- `dde.grad.jacobian` and `hessian` are documented as "lazy evaluation, i.e., it only computes J[i, j] when needed" with caching of already-computed gradients; a `config.autodiff == "forward"` switch routes to a separate `gradients_forward` module. **[DOC]** — [DeepXDE gradients docs](https://deepxde.readthedocs.io/en/latest/modules/deepxde.gradients.html); [source](https://deepxde.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_modules/deepxde/gradients/gradients.html)

### Inferences
- The honest reference figure for "Taylor mode versus nested AD at second order" is about 2x after collapsing, and *below* 1x without it (JAX jet 1.5x slower than nesting). A claim of a large second-order speed-up therefore has to be attributed to something other than the AD mode: compilation, fusion, removal of framework overhead, or small-network CPU regime.
- All published numbers above are for GPU with wide networks (64 to 1024 units) or high input dimension. The regime of narrow networks, few inputs and CPU execution is unmeasured in this literature; n-TangentProp (24-unit layers) is the only small-network data point and it is on an A6000 GPU.
- Cao et al. (June 2026) already occupy "per-residual specialised forward kernels verified against AD". What they do not do, by their own statement: a specialised reverse (parameter-gradient) pass, native compiled code outside PyTorch/JAX, CPU targeting, or use of the Griewank-Walther activation recurrences as a design element. A novelty statement must be drawn on that line, and should cite this preprint.
- Dangel et al. state that neither `torch.compile` nor `jax.jit` recovers the collapsed form automatically, and that their own Taylor arithmetic uses Faà di Bruno rather than the cheaper recurrences. That is published support for the claim that existing compilers leave algebraic savings on the table.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the final venue of the Forward Laplacian paper. I believe it appeared in Nature Machine Intelligence in 2024 under a slightly different title and that "LapNet" is the name of the accompanying network, but neither was verified in this session; the arXiv page lists no journal.
- The ZCS and separable-PINN numbers come from abstracts and search summaries; I did not read their experiment tables (hardware model for SPINN not captured).
- No source found for "hand-derived derivative networks" as a named line of work beyond n-TangentProp and Cao et al.; older analytic-derivative PINN papers may exist that these searches missed.
- DeepXDE publishes no timing for its forward-mode option that I could find.
- I did not read the Griewank and Walther book or the Bettencourt et al. workshop paper directly; both are cited through secondary sources.

## (A2) Compiled or code-generating approaches, and published CPU step-time comparisons across frameworks

### Takeaway
Compilation of PINN derivative code today means XLA through `jax.jit` (works, but does not apply Taylor-mode algebraic rewrites) or `torch.compile` (no speed gain measured for these operators, and its AOTAutograd path rejects double backward). I found no published cross-framework comparison of PINN step time on CPU; the comparisons that exist are whole-training wall times on GPU.

### Cited Findings
- XLA/JAX: for a stacked-backward baseline, moving from PyTorch to JAX gave "more than 10× faster" at high dimension, attributed to XLA JIT; but JIT raised peak memory enough to cause an out-of-memory failure at 100,000 dimensions. (full text read) **[PR]** — [Shi et al., arXiv 2412.00088](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.00088)
- XLA/JAX: for the Laplacian of a wide MLP on GPU, the framework made no difference to nested AD (PyTorch eager 0.61 ms per datum versus JAX jit 0.58 ms); "the choice of the ML framework does not affect the results". **[PR]** — [Dangel et al., arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- XLA/JAX: absolute JAX times are "substantially lower" than PyTorch for a 6 × 64 PINN because "XLA compilation can fuse operations". **[PP]** — [Cao et al., arXiv 2606.29702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29702)
- `torch.compile`: users receive the runtime error "torch.compile with aot_autograd does not currently support double backward"; a PyTorch developer reply says earlier efforts were dropped as hard and not top priority; tracking issue pytorch/pytorch #91469. Compiled Autograd (PyTorch 2.4+) captures a larger backward graph, but I found no confirmation that it supports double backward. **[DOC]** — [PyTorch forum](https://discuss.pytorch.org/t/double-backward-in-torch2-0/175454); [second thread](https://discuss.pytorch.org/t/torch-compile-emits-double-backward-error-even-when-the-corresponding-function-is-disabled/218952); [Compiled Autograd tutorial](https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/intermediate/compiled_autograd_tutorial.html)
- `torch.compile` applied to forward (Taylor) schemes: memory down, runtime "roughly identical"; unchanged for the biharmonic. **[PR]** — [Dangel et al., arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- Julia NeuralPDE.jl: training code "is generated from an abstract formulation" (symbolic ModelingToolkit interface). A user inspecting the generated loss found a closure around `NeuralPDE.numeric_derivative`, i.e. numerical differentiation of the network with respect to coordinates in that path (one user's observation, not documentation of the default). **[PP/DOC]** — [NeuralPDE paper, arXiv 2107.09443](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.09443.pdf); [Julia Discourse](https://discourse.julialang.org/t/loss-function-in-neuralpde-jl/109702)
- Julia TaylorDiff.jl: forum reports that it "probably is not compatible with Flux.jl" and fails to compile on networks; nested AD for PINNs in Julia is described as awkward. Lux documents "efficient nested AD upto 2nd order" and has a 2D PINN tutorial using Reactant and Enzyme (a demo, not a benchmark). **[DOC]** — [Discourse: higher-order AD](https://discourse.julialang.org/t/higher-order-derivatives-automatic-differentiation/107936); [Discourse: nested AD for PINNs](https://discourse.julialang.org/t/is-it-possible-to-do-nested-ad-elegantly-in-julia-pinns/98888); [Lux PINN tutorial](https://lux.csail.mit.edu/v1.6.0/tutorials/intermediate/4_PINN2DPDE)
- Enzyme: a 2025 MFEM workshop talk shows Enzyme-MLIR through Reactant.jl on a CUDA KAN network with 2.14x (primal) and 13.57x (derivative) speed-ups; a 2025 preprint benchmarks Enzyme.jl, ForwardDiff.jl and TaylorDiff.jl on the 20th derivative of sin x on Intel and Apple M3 CPUs (not a network). Neither is a PINN benchmark. — [MFEM workshop slides](https://mfem.org/pdf/workshop25/17_Sathia_Enzyme.pdf); [arXiv 2506.11719](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.11719)
- CPU-first small networks: SimpleChains.jl, "Doing small network scientific machine learning in Julia 5x faster than PyTorch" (Julia blog, April 2022); JuliaCon 2022 talk claims ">5x faster than Flux or Pytorch while all use a CPU" at tens of thousands of parameters, on an Intel i9-10980XE with AVX-512. These are first-order training benchmarks, not PINN residuals; Lux's own tutorial reports a smaller 3-4x. **[VENDOR-like: project's own blog]** — [Julia blog](https://www.julialang.org/blog/2022/04/simple-chains); [JuliaCon talk](https://pretalx.com/juliacon-2022/talk/9RFTHY/); [Lux tutorial](https://lux.csail.mit.edu/v1.9.0/tutorials/beginner/4_SimpleChains)
- Hand-written or generated kernels: Cao et al. generate tangent and transpose kernels for a Fortran GPU finite-volume solver and PyTorch/JAX layer kernels for PINNs (see A1). ACORNS generates C99 gradient and Hessian code "around an order of magnitude faster than existing methods" for general functions, not networks. — [arXiv 2606.29702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29702); [ACORNS, arXiv 2007.05094](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.05094)
- Cross-framework PINN timings that do exist (all GPU, whole training): jinns appendix, Burgers 1D on an NVIDIA T600: jinns 445 s, DeepXDE-JAX 723 s, DeepXDE-PyTorch 671 s, PINA 1977 s, NVIDIA Modulus 646 s. **[PP]** — [jinns, arXiv 2412.14132](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.14132v1)
- Akbarian Bafghi and Raissi, "Comparing PINNs Across Frameworks: JAX, TensorFlow, and PyTorch" (ICLR 2024 workshop): JAX wins on simpler examples, TensorFlow V2 may win on large ones such as 3D Navier-Stokes; speed-ups are plotted relative to the original TensorFlow V1 code. Their PINNs-TF2 paper reports an average 18.12x and a peak 119.96x over TensorFlow V1. **[WS]** — [ICLR workshop page](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2024/21394); [pinns-jax repo](https://github.com/rezaakb/pinns-jax); [PINNs-TF2, arXiv 2311.03626](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.03626)

### Inferences
- A CPU step-time table across PyTorch, `torch.func`, JAX jit, `jax.experimental.jet` and a native kernel on the same PINN would be new published data; I found nothing equivalent.
- The two strongest published statements about compilers are negative ones: jit does not find the collapsed form, and `torch.compile` does not speed up forward schemes and cannot compile nested reverse mode. A compiled engine can cite both as the reason generic compilation is not enough.
- Where framework choice did not matter (Dangel et al.) the network was wide and on GPU, so matrix products dominated. Where compilation mattered (Cao et al., Schettini Gherardini) the network was small and per-operation overhead dominated. That contrast is the published basis for expecting the largest gains in the small-network regime.

### Gaps
- No source found on Tapenade-style source transformation applied to PINN residuals, nor on SymPy code generation of PINN residual kernels with benchmarks.
- No peer-reviewed benchmark of NeuralPDE.jl against Python frameworks was found; the NeuralPDE default derivative strategy was not confirmed from documentation.
- Patrick Kidger's gist comparing JAX with SimpleChains exists ([gist](https://gist.github.com/patrick-kidger/68bf7b99ba02c246b20eaa38f2ad3d38)) but I did not retrieve its timings.
- Current status of PyTorch issue #91469 (double backward under `torch.compile`) in the latest release was not checked.

## (A3) What is not available in existing tools

### Takeaway
Three things were not found in any published tool or paper: a reverse pass generated per equation (all forward schemes hand the parameter gradient back to generic reverse-mode AD), activation derivative recurrences fused into the layer kernel (the leading Taylor implementation says it does not use them), and CPU-first measurements for small PINNs. Absence here means "not found in about 30 searches", not proof of absence.

### Cited Findings
- Parameter gradients left to generic AD: Cao et al., "parameter gradients for training are still computed by reverse-mode differentiation of the scalar loss". — [arXiv 2606.29702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29702)
- Backward pass is the weak side of forward schemes: in n-TangentProp, autodiff "outperformed" the method on backward passes for small derivative counts, and gains are "larger in the forward pass". — [arXiv 2412.09752](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09752v1)
- Differentiable forward schemes cost memory: Dangel et al. separate "non-differentiable" from "differentiable" peak memory because training "demands saving intermediates". — [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- Recurrences unused: Dangel et al. "do not implement the efficient schemes from Griewank & Walther [13, §13], but stick to Faà di Bruno"; Taylor arithmetic must be "implemented case by case" per primitive. — [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- Compilers do not do it for you: "JAX's jit compiler is unable to apply the graph rewrites we propose"; future work "could focus on integrating these optimizations directly into ML compilers". — [arXiv 2505.13644v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)
- Hardware in every benchmark located: RTX 6000 (Dangel), A100 (Shi), A6000 (Chickering), RTX 5080 (Cao), T600 (jinns). No CPU PINN step times. — sources as in A1 and A2.
- Specialisation cost acknowledged: explicit kernels must be "regenerated and reverified" when architecture, activation or residual change; the method "does not provide black-box differentiation". — [arXiv 2606.29702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29702)

### Inferences
- A defensible novelty statement for a compiled per-equation Taylor-mode CPU engine: (1) the reverse pass through the jet propagation is itself generated per equation rather than delegated to autograd; (2) activation jets come from fused recurrences rather than Faà di Bruno tables; (3) native code with measured CPU step times for small networks. Items (1) and (3) are directly contrasted by quotations above; item (2) is supported for PyTorch-side tools only, since I did not inspect how `jax.experimental.jet` implements tanh.
- What is *not* new and should be credited: Taylor mode itself (Griewank-Walther; Bettencourt et al.), propagating only the derivatives the operator needs (forward Laplacian, collapsed Taylor mode, DOF), and residual-specific kernels verified against AD (Cao et al., four months before this note).
- Speed-up claims should be stated against both nested reverse mode and a jit-compiled Taylor baseline, since Cao et al. show the second is 1.3-1.8x faster than the first on the same problems.

### Gaps
- Whether `jax.experimental.jet` uses recurrences for tanh and similar primitives was not checked in source.
- I did not search commercial codes (NVIDIA PhysicsNeMo/Modulus internals, Ansys, Siemens) for native PINN kernels; Modulus appears only as a timing row in the jinns table.

## (B1) Published industrial or pilot-scale applications in dairy and food processing: evaporators, spray drying, fouling, soft sensors

### Takeaway
What is running on real dairy plants is classical: first-principles or grey-box models with MPC and inferential sensors (GEA with DTU, Rockwell at Miraka). Hybrid models validated on pilot or industrial evaporator data appeared in 2026 from DTU and GEA. I found no PINN validated on a spray dryer or falling-film evaporator with plant data; the nearest PINN "digital twin" of a dryer is for a surgical-instrument dryer.

### Cited Findings

Spray drying
- Petersen (DTU PhD thesis 2016, industrial PhD with GEA Process Engineering, supervisors Jørgensen, Niemann, Utzen, Poulsen): MPC with real-time optimisation on an industrial GEA MSD-1250 four-stage spray dryer, about 7,500 kg/h, enriched milk powder. First industrial results versus conventional PI control: profit about 228,000 €/year higher, product rate +322 kg/h, residual moisture closer to the limit by 0.166 percentage points, energy efficiency +1 %, at comparable ambient humidity; the controller is "fully integrated in the daily operation". ### Inferences
- A simulated-plant PINN result for evaporators or spray dryers is not behind the academic state of the art in being simulated: almost nobody has plant-validated PINNs here. It is behind the industrial state of the art, which is plant-validated grey-box models with MPC that have run in daily operation since about 2016.
- The active group to position against (or approach) is DTU Chemical Engineering with GEA Process Engineering: they hold industrial evaporator data, pilot evaporator data and the spray dryer MPC lineage. Nestlé with TUHH is the visible spray-drying hybrid effort.
- The GEA and DTU fouling result (mechanistic layer model predicts, empirical loss model does not) is direct published support for physics-structured fouling estimation over purely fitted correction factors.
- Benefits in this sector are quoted in small percentages (1 % energy, 0.04 % yield, 0.166 points of moisture, 4 % capacity). A twin's uncertainty has to be tighter than these margins to matter commercially.

## (B2) What industry requires before adoption

### Takeaway
The recurring requirements in the literature are lifecycle ones, not accuracy ones: validation in shadow mode on historian data, drift monitoring and re-qualification under change control, integration through OPC UA and ISA-95 layers with historians and the DCS, explicit uncertainty, and documented model governance. In regulated manufacturing the current European draft restricts AI in critical GMP uses to static, deterministic models.

### Cited Findings

Lifecycle, validation, maintenance
- Chew, Andersson, Bierweiler, Ryttestal, Wik, "AI in chemical engineering: From promise to practice", AIChE Journal 72(7), 2026 (full text read): "The decisive bottleneck is reliable deployment: AI models must be treated like any other engineered system, with validation, monitoring, and governance". **[PR]** — [Chalmers open copy](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/551339/file/551339_Fulltext.pdf)
- Same review: models "must be managed as engineering lifecycles, requiring commissioning, monitoring, drift detection, re-qualification, and redeployment, with documented tests and sign-offs". — [Chalmers open copy](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/551339/file/551339_Fulltext.pdf)
- Same review, recommended sequence: commissioning in "shadow" mode "where models are validated using historian data and accompanied by a published Model Card describing intended use and limitations", then deployment "behind safety layers"; retraining triggered by "drift, MOC event, equipment change". — [Chalmers open copy](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/551339/file/551339_Fulltext.pdf)
- Same review on data: industrial data are "sparse, noisy, and unevenly distributed across operating regimes", with "sensor drift, calibration errors, unlogged manual interventions, and limited fault data". — [Chalmers open copy](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/551339/file/551339_Fulltext.pdf)
- Same review: "gray-box modeling should be prioritized where feasible, complemented by formal V&V"; and "large-scale industrial deployment remains limited" for hybrid approaches, with autonomous-operation claims "described in vendor-reported case studies". — [Chalmers open copy](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/551339/file/551339_Fulltext.pdf)

Incumbent tools to compare against
- Economic MPC "typically resides within a two-layer hierarchy" with RTO above regulatory control (Chew et al.); in dairy the deployed incumbents are first-principles MPC with RTO (GEA and DTU) and Pavilion8 MPC with inferential models (Rockwell). — [Chew et al. 2026](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/551339/file/551339_Fulltext.pdf); [DTU Orbit](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/economic-model-predictive-control-for-spray-drying-plants/); [Rockwell](https://www.rockwellautomation.com/en-au/company/news/case-studies/miraka-benefits-from-model-predictive-control-solution.html)

### Inferences
- A checklist from simulated plant to industry-ready, in the order the literature implies: (1) offline validation on historian data from a real unit, across operating regimes and a full fouling and cleaning cycle; (2) shadow-mode operation with a model card; (3) calibrated uncertainty and input-validity checks, because sensors drift and upsets are under-represented; (4) a maintenance protocol with drift detection and re-qualification triggers tied to management of change; (5) OPC UA read access through a NOA-style side channel and historian connectors, advisory first, closed loop only behind the existing safety and MPC layers; (6) a benchmark against the incumbent grey-box model plus Kalman filter or MPC inferential, not against a neural baseline.
- A twin that re-estimates parameters online is, for GMP-regulated products, at odds with the "static, deterministic" direction of draft Annex 22; estimating physical parameters of a fixed model (state and parameter estimation) is easier to defend than retraining network weights in production.
- Food dairy is not under pharmaceutical GMP, but infant formula and pharma spray drying are held to comparable expectations, so the pharma documents are the stricter reference.

- No source retrieved on AVEVA PI System (or other historian) integration specifics, nor on MQTT/Sparkplug in process plants; the claim that these are the usual historians and transports is not sourced here.
- The exact ICH Q13 wording on low, medium and high impact models and their validation was not retrieved; neither was the PAT guidance's own text on multivariate models and real-time release.
- No food-specific regulatory source (FSMA, HACCP, EU hygiene rules) on model-based control was found or searched in depth.
- No source directly comparing a PINN or hybrid twin with Aspen or gPROMS models, or with vendor Kalman-filter soft sensors, on the same dairy unit.
- Explainability requirements were only found as general statements (model interpretability "for regulatory compliance" in a 2026 narrative review: [Chemical and Process Engineering](https://wydawnictwo.pan.pl/index.php/cpe/article/view/2670)).

## (B3) Documented failures or cautions about PINNs and ML digital twins in process industries

### Takeaway
The documented cautions are of two kinds: methodological (PINNs fail to train on convection, reaction and stiff problems, do not beat classical solvers on cost or accuracy, and are often compared with weak baselines) and operational (most industrial AI pilots never reach production; maintenance, not accuracy, sinks soft sensors). I found no published post-mortem of a failed PINN deployment in a process plant, which itself reflects how few have been attempted.

### Cited Findings
- Krishnapriyan, Gholami, Zhe, Kirby, Mahoney, "Characterizing possible failure modes in physics-informed neural networks", NeurIPS 2021: standard PINNs "can easily fail to learn relevant physical phenomena for even slightly more complex problems" with convection, reaction and diffusion; the cause is a loss landscape that is hard to optimise; curriculum and sequence-to-sequence training cut error by up to 1-2 orders of magnitude. **[PR]** — [arXiv 2109.01050](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01050)
- Wang, Yu, Perdikaris, "When and why PINNs fail to train: A neural tangent kernel perspective" (arXiv 2020; J. Comput. Phys.): "a remarkable discrepancy in the convergence rate of the different loss components". **[PR]** — [arXiv 2007.14527](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14527)
- Grossmann, Komorowska, Latz, Schönlieb, "Can physics-informed neural networks beat the finite element method?", IMA J. Appl. Math. 89(1), 2024: "In terms of solution time and accuracy, physics-informed neural networks have not been able to outperform the finite element method in our study" (Poisson, Allen-Cahn, semilinear Schrödinger); PINNs were sometimes faster to *evaluate* once trained. **[PR]** — [PMC11197852](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11197852/)
- McGreivy and Hakim, "Weak baselines and reporting biases lead to overoptimism in machine learning for fluid-related partial differential equations", Nature Machine Intelligence 6(10), 2024: of articles claiming to outperform a standard numerical method, "79% (60/76) make a comparison with a weak baseline"; reporting biases suppress negative results. **[PR]** — [arXiv 2407.07218](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07218); [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/weak-baselines-and-reporting-biases-lead-to-overoptimism-in-machi/)
- Chew et al. 2026: "PINNs can suffer from convergence and stability" problems; standard PINNs are trained for specific boundary conditions and need retraining, "which restricts their applicability in tasks such as control". **[PR]** — [Chalmers open copy](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/551339/file/551339_Fulltext.pdf)
- Mukherjee and Zavala, "Physics-Constrained Machine Learning for Chemical Engineering" (2025 preprint): open challenges are closed-loop experimental design, real-time dynamics and control, and multi-scale phenomena. **[PP]** — [arXiv 2508.20649](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20649v1)
- A 2026 narrative review lists barriers: "data quality and standardization, model interpretability for regulatory compliance, computational scalability for real-time control, and integration with legacy industrial infrastructure". **[PR, narrative]** — [Chemical and Process Engineering](https://wydawnictwo.pan.pl/index.php/cpe/article/view/2670)
- Purely empirical correction models fail for prediction: the GEA and DTU fouling study found the empirical heat-transfer-loss model "cannot be applied for model-based prediction or control". **[PR, proceedings]** — [LAPSE:2026.0368](https://psecommunity.org/LAPSE:2026.0368v1)
- Pilot-to-production attrition: 72 % of organisations with pre-2019 AI pilots had deployed nothing to production (Google Cloud 2021, via Chew et al.); 68 % still in pilots (HiveMQ 2026, vendor survey). — [Chew et al. 2026](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/551339/file/551339_Fulltext.pdf); [HiveMQ](https://www.hivemq.com/blog/industrial-ai-pilot-why-68-percent-manufacturers-cant-scale-past-poc/)
- AD-specific caution: Mikulski and Uhl (2026 preprint) argue that the standard PyTorch autograd idiom for coordinate derivatives is "silently incorrect" for architectures with inter-sample dependencies such as BatchNorm and self-attention. **[PP]** — [arXiv 2608.11020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11020)

### Inferences
- A project reporting that a PINN twin fails on a share of cases and that classical estimators win is consistent with the peer-reviewed record (Grossmann et al.; McGreivy and Hakim) and is a publishable negative result precisely because such results are under-reported.
- For process units with a cheap forward solve, the literature gives no reason to expect a PINN to beat a mechanistic model with a moving-horizon or Kalman estimator; the defensible niche is where the forward model is expensive or the twin must be amortised across conditions.
- Any speed claim for an engine should be accompanied by a strong classical baseline, given how explicitly the weak-baseline problem has been documented.

### Gaps
- No published account of a PINN or ML digital twin being withdrawn from a food or dairy plant was found; failures are probably unpublished.
- I did not retrieve the stiff-kinetics PINN literature (for example Ji et al. on stiff chemical kinetics), which is directly relevant to drying and fouling kinetics.
- Vendor survey percentages come from differing samples and definitions and are not specific to the process industries.
