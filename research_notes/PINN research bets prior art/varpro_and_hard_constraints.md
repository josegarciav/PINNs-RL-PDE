# Exact linear output layer (VarPro / ELM / random features) and hard-constrained ansatz for PINNs

Reading conventions used below. "PR" = peer-reviewed venue confirmed in this session; "preprint" = arXiv only as far as I could confirm. Numbers are copied from the papers' own tables (arXiv versions, read from the PDF text) with their setup. Timings are the authors' own, on the hardware and software they state; none are comparable across papers.

## (A-1) Key papers on solving the linear output layer exactly: what accuracy and timing do they report, on which equations, against FEM and against standard PINNs?

### Takeaway
For smooth low-dimensional problems, fixing random hidden weights and solving the output layer by (non)linear least squares gives errors of 1e-8 to 1e-12 in seconds on a laptop CPU, versus 1e-3 to 1e-5 in hundreds to thousands of seconds for Adam/L-BFGS PINNs; against FEM the authors' own tables show parity with or advantage over second-order FEniCS, and a crossover against high-order FEM. Variable projection of trained hidden weights (Dong and Yang 2022) gains several orders of magnitude over ELM at small width but costs 25 to 250 times more training time in their table.

### Cited Findings

**PIELM (Dwivedi and Srinivasan; arXiv July 2019, journal version Neurocomputing 2020)**
- Single hidden layer, tanh, random fixed input weights, output weights from one linear least-squares solve; linear PDEs only (stationary and time dependent). All experiments in Matlab 2017b on an Intel Core i5 2.20 GHz, 8 GB laptop. — [arXiv 1907.03507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03507)
- 1D steady cases: TC-1 and TC-2 (advection, diffusion from Berg and Nyström) with [Nf, Nbc, N*] = [40, 2, 42] reach error O(1e-4); TC-3 [20, 2, 22] reaches O(1e-6). The authors note the Berg–Nyström deep network "took 100 points to achieve an order of accuracy of 1e-5 and 1e-3 in TC-1 and TC-2". — [arXiv 1907.03507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03507)
- 2D steady cases: TC-4 and TC-5 with [921, 240, 2000] reach O(1e-6) and O(1e-4); TC-6 (diffusion on a complex polygon, map of Illinois) with [1489, 881, 5000] reaches O(1e-7) "with just 2370 points ... in just 10 seconds". The deep-network baseline of Berg and Nyström "took 5500 points to achieve an order of accuracy of 1e-3 in TC-4 and TC-5". — [arXiv 1907.03507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03507)
- 1D unsteady case with Nf = 420, Nbc = 21, Nic = 20, N* = 440: "total learning time is within 2-3 seconds". — [arXiv 1907.03507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03507)
- Documented failures of the single global PIELM: advection of a sharp Gaussian fails even with 250x30 points and 7780 neurons; the 2D unsteady advection-diffusion case used 125000 points and 1000 neurons and was still diffusive with unphysical oscillations. A 9x20 tanh deep PINN also failed on advection of a high-frequency wave packet. The authors' remedy is a distributed version (DPIELM) with interface continuity conditions. — [arXiv 1907.03507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03507)
- The authors explicitly decline to claim superiority over classical solvers: "a firm conclusion on this cannot be reached until a more thorough and fair study is done", and they restrict the paper to linear problems. — [arXiv 1907.03507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03507)

**locELM (Dong and Li, CMAME 387 (2021) 114129; PR)**
- Local shallow random-weight networks per sub-domain, C^k continuity across sub-domain boundaries, output weights by linear least squares (linear PDEs) or nonlinear least squares (nonlinear PDEs); block time marching for long time horizons. TensorFlow/Keras plus SciPy; FEM baseline in FEniCS; all timings on a Mac, 3.2 GHz Intel Core i5, 24 GB. FEM time excludes JIT compilation. — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895); [CMAME PDF](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sdong/PDF/locelm_CMAME2021.pdf)
- 1D Helmholtz (Table 1): PINN/Adam max error 1.06e-3, rms 1.57e-4, 45,000 epochs, 507.7 s; PINN/L-BFGS 1.98e-4, 3.15e-5, 22,500 iterations, 1035.8 s; locELM 1.56e-9, 2.25e-10, 1.1 s. — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)
- 1D Helmholtz versus FEM (Table 2): locELM with 4 sub-domains, Q = 100, M = 75/100/125 gives max error 4.02e-8 / 1.56e-9 / 1.42e-10 in 0.67 / 1.1 / 1.3 s; FEM with 25,000 / 50,000 / 100,000 elements gives 6.82e-8 / 1.67e-8 / 1.33e-8 in 0.32 / 0.62 / 1.24 s. — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)
- Advection equation (Table 3): DGM/Adam 8.37e-3 max, 2527.8 s (60,000 epochs); DGM/L-BFGS 2.59e-3, 1675.9 s; locELM without block time marching 2.74e-4, 43.4 s; with block time marching (2 blocks, 8 sub-domains each) 1.83e-4, 19.3 s. — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)
- 1D diffusion (Tables 4 and 5): DGM/Adam 2.59e-2 max in 4194.5 s; DGM/L-BFGS 5.82e-3 in 3201.4 s; locELM 5.82e-8 in 28.4 s (5 sub-domains, Q = 30x30, M = 300). FEM (BDF2 in time) with 10000 elements and dt = 0.00025 gives 4.24e-6 in 179.0 s; locELM with Q = 20x20, M = 250 gives 8.97e-8 in 11.3 s. — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)
- Nonlinear Helmholtz (Tables 6 and 7): PINN/Adam 4.56e-3 max, 578.2 s; PINN/L-BFGS 1.69e-3, 806.4 s; locELM NLSQ-perturb 1.45e-9, 71 nonlinear iterations, 7.7 s; locELM Newton-LLSQ 1.28e-5, 5 iterations, 2.7 s. FEM with 200k / 400k / 800k elements: 5.26e-9 / 1.31e-9 / 3.29e-10 in 4.7 / 8.8 / 18.1 s; locELM (Q = 125, M = 200) 3.96e-11 in 10.6 s. — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)
- Nonlinear spring ODE (Table 8): PINN/Adam 1.21e-4 max, 20,000 epochs, 26.3 s; locELM NLSQ-perturb 2.82e-11, 48 iterations, 0.34 s. — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)
- Viscous Burgers (Tables 9 and 10): DGM/Adam 4.57e-2 max, 1797.8 s; DGM/L-BFGS 7.50e-3, 1813.5 s; locELM NLSQ-perturb 1.85e-8, 27 iterations, 27.6 s; locELM Newton-LLSQ 1.62e-5, 15 iterations, 9.1 s. FEM best case (10000 elements, dt = 0.0005) 4.74e-6 max in 92.6 s; FEM 2000 elements, dt = 0.001: 2.64e-5 in 12.5 s. — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)
- 2D Helmholtz (Table 11): PINN/Adam 4.13e-2 max, 107,000 epochs, 7213.7 s; PINN/L-BFGS 1.27e-2, 5051.2 s; global ELM 4.17e-5 in 410.6 s; locELM with 4 sub-domains 2.01e-5 in 33.6 s. — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)

**ELM hyperparameter and FEM comparison (Dong and Yang; arXiv 2110.14121; arXiv page lists DOI 10.1016/j.jcp.2022.111290, i.e. J. Comput. Phys. 2022; PR)**
- The optimal scale Rm of the random hidden coefficients is computed by differential evolution (single-Rm and multi-Rm variants); differential operators on the last hidden layer are computed with forward-mode autodiff instead of reverse mode, which cuts training time. — [arXiv 2110.14121](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14121)
- The authors restate the earlier locELM result less favourably than its abstract: locELM "can ... outperform the Lagrange elements of degree 2, but can barely outperform the Lagrange elements of degree 3" and "cannot outcompete high-order FEM with element degrees 4 or larger". — [arXiv 2110.14121](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.14121)
- With the improvements, the claim is: ELM "far outperforms the classical FEM in essentially all cases, except for a narrow range with very small problem sizes (FEM mesh size below around 50 x 50, ELM collocation points below around 13 x 13; error level above around 5e-2; wall time below around 0.03 seconds)"; versus high-order FEM (Lagrange degree 3 to 6) there is a crossover, high-order FEM "a little better" for small sizes and ELM markedly better for large sizes; for time-dependent PDEs ELM with block time marching "consistently and far outperforms" both. Results are given as figures (2D Poisson and others), not tables. Same Mac i5 3.2 GHz, FEniCS baseline. — [arXiv 2110.14121](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.14121)

**Random feature method, RFM (Chen, Chi, E, Yang; J. Machine Learning 1(3), 2022, 268-298; PR)**
- Random feature functions blended by a partition of unity, collocation, penalty treatment of boundary conditions with an automatic per-row rescaling so that all loss terms have the same magnitude; linear least-squares solve. — [arXiv 2207.13380](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13380); [journal page](https://global-sci.org/intro/article_detail/jml/21029.html)
- As extracted by the fetch tool from the paper's tables (I did not re-read these tables myself): 1D Helmholtz, M = 1600 random features, L-infinity error 8.84e-12 versus a PINN at about 8.94e-3; 2D Poisson down to 1.66e-11 (N = 28160 conditions); Timoshenko beam 8.16e-12 for u (M = 800, N = 14400) with locELM quoted at about 1e-3; Stokes 2.45e-13 (u) and 1.37e-9 (p) at M = 1600, N = 19488. No timing results are reported; the paper names scipy's least-squares as the cost bottleneck. — [ar5iv 2207.13380](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.13380)
- The same extraction flags an internal inconsistency in the complex-geometry elasticity comparisons (text says RFM–FEM difference "about 1%", table shows roughly 3.3–4.0 %; text says "about 5%", table shows about 7 %). Treat those two comparisons as approximate. — [ar5iv 2207.13380](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.13380)

**Variable projection for PDEs (Dong and Yang, CMAME 398 (2022) 115284; PR)**
- Output-layer coefficients eliminated by linear least squares; reduced problem in the hidden-layer coefficients solved by `scipy.optimize.least_squares` (Gauss-Newton with trust-region reflective), with the Kaufman approximate Jacobian (first term of Golub–Pereyra only) and random perturbation restarts. Networks with one or more hidden layers. — [arXiv 2201.09989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09989); [CMAME PDF](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sdong/PDF/varpro_cmame2022.pdf)
- 2D Poisson, network [2, 100, 1], cos activation, Rm = 1 (Table 2): at 15x15 collocation points VarPro max error 6.018e-7 versus ELM 1.475e+1; at 30x30, VarPro 2.688e-7 versus ELM 1.864e+1. With ELM's optimal Rm0 = 6: VarPro 8.709e-8 versus ELM 4.673e-1 at 30x30. Network [2, 200, 1], Rm = 6, 20x20: VarPro 3.449e-10 versus ELM 1.245e-5. — [arXiv 2201.09989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09989)
- Burgers, [2, M, 1], Q = 31x31, Rm = 1 (Table 8): M = 50, VarPro 1.538e-5 versus ELM 6.669e-2; M = 150, VarPro 1.131e-8 versus ELM 4.387e-6. — [arXiv 2201.09989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09989)
- Cost (Table 10): advection, [2, 100, 1], 10x10 / 15x15 / 20x20 points: VarPro 7.4 / 15.4 / 78.2 s versus ELM 0.29 / 0.31 / 0.32 s. Nonlinear Helmholtz, [2, 200, 1]: VarPro 1.7 / 36.5 / 78.1 s versus ELM 2.0 / 2.8 / 4.0 s. The authors: VarPro cost "is generally much higher than that of the ELM method"; VarPro is "less sensitive or insensitive" to Rm than ELM. — [arXiv 2201.09989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09989)
- The paper compares against ELM only; it contains no FEM or PINN wall-clock comparison. — [arXiv 2201.09989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09989)

**VarPro for general neural networks**
- O'Leary and Rust, "Variable projection for nonlinear least squares problems", Comput. Optim. Appl. 54(3), 579-593 (2013), DOI 10.1007/s10589-012-9492-9; a robust implementation of the Golub–Pereyra (1973) algorithm for separable least squares. PR. — [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:spr:coopap:v:54:y:2013:i:3:p:579-593); [paper PDF](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/oleary/software/varpro.pdf)
- Newman, Ruthotto, Hart, van Bloemen Waanders, "Train Like a (Var)Pro", SIAM J. Math. Data Sci. 3(4), 2021, DOI 10.1137/20M1359511 (PR): Gauss-Newton–Krylov trust-region VarPro (GNvpro) for any network whose last layer is affine, extended to cross-entropy. PDE surrogate regression (not PINN residual training), mean relative test error (Table 2): convection-diffusion-reaction, GNvpro 0.0060 ± 0.0032 at 600 work units versus full Gauss-Newton 0.0142 ± 0.0072 (600 WU) and Adam 0.0116 ± 0.0072 (1200 WU); DC resistivity, GNvpro 0.0199 ± 0.0322 versus full GN 0.3517 ± 0.6638 and Adam 0.2904 ± 0.3256. In the segmentation example the inner linear solve took "only 6.4% of the total training time". — [arXiv 2007.13171](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.13171); [author page](https://math.emory.edu/~lruthot/publication/newman-et-al-2020)
- Dus, "Grassmannian Geometry and Global Convergence of Variable Projection for Neural Networks" (arXiv Jan 2026; preprint): analyses critical points and convergence of the reduced VarPro problem for deep networks and PINNs, with a regularized formulation for rank-deficient regimes; experiments on "simple toy problems" in TensorFlow CPU mode on an Intel Core i5-10300H; 1D heat equation solved as a sequence of implicit-Euler VarPro solves with L2 error staying below about 1e-2 up to T = 0.15. — [arXiv 2601.22897](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.22897)

**Hybrid schemes: least squares for the last layer, gradient or Gauss-Newton for the rest**
- Cyr, Gulian, Patel, Perego, Trask, "Robust Training and Initialization of Deep Neural Networks: An Adaptive Basis Viewpoint", MSML 2020, PMLR 107:512-536 (PR, conference): LSGD alternates a least-squares solve for output weights with gradient-descent steps on hidden weights; reported loss "typically several orders of magnitude lower" than plain GD after the same number of iterations on regression, and a PINN test on the linear transport equation (shallow ReLU net, width 32) comparing GD after 5000 iterations with LSGD after 500. — [PMLR page](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v107/cyr20a.html); [arXiv 1912.04862](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.04862)
- Abedi, Pardo, Alkhalifah, "Least-Squares-Embedded Optimization for Accelerated Convergence of PINNs in Acoustic Wavefield Simulations" (arXiv April 2025; preprint): the LS solve for the linear output layer is embedded in the loss and differentiated through; hidden layers trained by Adam; Tikhonov damping about 0.1 early, reduced to about 1e-4; Cholesky on a PxP (or 2Px2P with PML) normal-equation system. On a 30 Hz Marmousi Helmholtz problem the standard PINN "still had not reached the accuracy LS-GD achieved in 15,000 epochs, even after 150,000 epochs". Soft constraints only. They cite Uriarte et al. (2025) for the variational-PINN version and Baharlouei et al. (2025) for LS-Net. — [arXiv 2504.16553](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16553v1)
- TransNet (Zhang, Bao, Ju, Zhang; arXiv 2301.11701; journal version reported as J. Sci. Comput. 2024, DOI 10.1007/s10915-024-02463-y): hidden-layer features are constructed once, independent of the PDE, and only the output layer is solved. — [arXiv 2301.11701](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.11701)
- ELM-FBPINNs (Anderson, Dolean, Moseley, Pestana; Machine Learning for Computational Science and Engineering (2026) 2:23; PR): overlapping-domain-decomposition FBPINN with ELM sub-networks, so training is a linear algebra problem, plus a multilevel extension. — [arXiv 2409.01949](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.01949v2); [journal PDF](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/96346/13/Anderson-etal-MLCSE-2026-ELM-FBPINNs-An-efficient-multilevel-random-feature-method.pdf)
- Fabiani, Calabrò, Russo, Siettos, J. Sci. Comput. 89:44 (2021) (PR): ELM collocation with Newton on the output weights for steady nonlinear PDEs and bifurcation diagrams (viscous Burgers, Liouville–Bratu–Gelfand); reported to outperform finite differences and FEM in accuracy for medium to large grids. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10915-021-01650-5); [arXiv 2104.06116](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06116)
- Galaris, Fabiani, Calabrò, di Serafino, Siettos (arXiv 2108.01584): random-projection network with Gauss-Newton on the output weights and adaptive time intervals for stiff IVPs (Prothero–Robinson, van der Pol, ROBER, HIRES), compared with MATLAB ode45 and ode15s; "computational times of our approach are comparable with those of the two MATLAB solvers for practical purposes". — [arXiv 2108.01584](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.01584)

### Inferences
- The accuracy gap between ELM-family methods and gradient-trained PINNs (5 to 9 orders of magnitude in the Dong tables) is measured on smooth manufactured solutions in 1D, 1D+time and 2D with TensorFlow-based PINN baselines trained by Adam/L-BFGS. None of these papers compares against second-order PINN trainers (Gauss-Newton, Levenberg–Marquardt, natural gradient), which are known to close part of that gap; so the published ratios overstate the advantage over a well-trained PINN.
- All FEM comparisons that favour ELM come from one group (Dong and co-authors) against FEniCS on a single Mac, mostly with manufactured smooth solutions that favour a spectrally converging method over a low-order one. The same group's own follow-up says the first paper's method only barely beat cubic elements. The honest reading: ELM is competitive with FEM for smooth solutions at high accuracy targets; nothing is shown for non-smooth solutions, boundary layers or shocks.
- Every reported timing in this literature is Python (TensorFlow, autograd, NumPy/SciPy LAPACK) or MATLAB. X-TFC states directly that its total run time (seconds to minutes) is dominated by autodiff overhead while the least-squares solve itself takes milliseconds (see B-1). A compiled engine with analytic derivatives attacks exactly the dominant cost, and no published number exists for that configuration.
- Dong and Yang's VarPro result is the strongest published argument for combining an exact last-layer solve with second-order training of hidden weights: it turns a width-100 network from useless (error 1e+1) into 1e-7. Its 25 to 250 times cost penalty comes from a generic SciPy trust-region solver with dense Jacobians via TensorFlow; it is an implementation-bound number, not an intrinsic one.

## (A-2) How are nonlinear PDEs handled, and what are the limits (ill-conditioning, random feature scale, domain decomposition, time dependence)?

### Takeaway
Nonlinearity is handled in three ways: nonlinear least squares directly on the output coefficients (Gauss-Newton / trust region, with random restarts), Newton linearization of the PDE followed by a linear least-squares solve per step, or time discretization that makes each step linear (IMEX). The documented limits are the ill-conditioned least-squares matrix, strong sensitivity to the random-feature scale, failure of a single global network on sharp or high-frequency solutions, and error growth across time blocks.

### Cited Findings
- locELM offers two nonlinear solvers. NLSQ-perturb: SciPy `least_squares` on the output coefficients, restarted from randomly perturbed initial guesses when the converged residual exceeds a threshold. Newton-LLSQ: Newton iteration with a linear least-squares solve per step. On the same problems NLSQ-perturb is 3 to 4 orders more accurate but 3 times slower: nonlinear Helmholtz 1.45e-9 (71 iterations, 7.7 s) versus 1.28e-5 (5 iterations, 2.7 s); Burgers 1.85e-8 (27.6 s) versus 1.62e-5 (9.1 s). — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)
- VarPro cannot be applied directly to a nonlinear PDE because the least-squares problem is no longer separable ("all the weight/bias coefficients in the neural network become nonlinear parameters, even if the output layer contains no activation function"). Dong and Yang's Newton-VarPro linearizes first, in terms of the updated field rather than the increment, which they report is critical to accuracy; at low resolution the Newton iteration "may have difficulty reaching convergence". — [arXiv 2201.09989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09989)
- X-TFC solves nonlinear problems by iterative least squares on the output weights (10 iterations in its two nonlinear PDE examples). — [arXiv 2005.10632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.10632)
- PIELM's authors named the two routes in 2019 as open work: solve the non-convex problem with the ELM structure, or linearize around the current time step. — [arXiv 1907.03507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03507)
- Tan, Yue, Chen, Jiang (arXiv Oct 2025; preprint): for large 3D nonlinear RFM problems the nonlinear least-squares systems are "extremely large and ill-conditioned"; they propose an inexact Newton method with right preconditioning built from randomized Jacobian compression and QR, LSQR inner solves, a derivative-free line search, and a multi-step variant that reuses the preconditioner. Tested on 3D steady elliptic, diffusion-reaction, Helmholtz and Gray–Scott problems and 2D time-dependent problems on moving geometries; they claim higher accuracy than FEM/FD and order-of-magnitude gains over PINN and WAN. — [arXiv 2510.04170](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.04170)
- A discrete-time RFM with implicit-explicit Runge–Kutta stepping was proposed in 2026 specifically to avoid the nonlinear least-squares problem for nonlinear evolution equations (search-result description only). — [arXiv 2604.25502](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.25502)
- FastLSQ (arXiv Feb 2026; preprint): frozen random Fourier features with analytical operator assembly, extended to nonlinear PDEs by Newton–Raphson (search-result description only). — [arXiv 2602.10541](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10541v1)

**Ill-conditioning**
- Dong and Yang: solving via normal equations "lead[s] to severe ill-conditioning and significantly lower accuracy, even if the original system is only moderately ill-conditioned"; they use LAPACK least-squares routines (SVD/QR-based) instead and choose Rm to avoid the rank-deficient regime. — [arXiv 2110.14121](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.14121)
- RFM authors acknowledge the least-squares formulation greatly enlarges the condition number and suggest preconditioning or reformulation as future work. — [ar5iv 2207.13380](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.13380)
- 2025 preprints report that RFMs "lead to highly ill-conditioned least squares systems" and propose sub-domain preconditioners from rank-revealing QR, or local feature filtering. — [arXiv 2506.17626](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.17626v2)
- Abedi et al. need Tikhonov damping of about 0.1 early in training, later reduced to about 1e-4, for the embedded LS solve to be stable when hidden layers are being trained. — [arXiv 2504.16553](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16553v1)
- Dus (2026) identifies rank-deficient feature maps during training as the regime where the VarPro reduced problem loses its good properties, and regularizes for it. — [arXiv 2601.22897](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.22897)

**Random feature scale**
- locELM: accuracy deteriorates for very large or very small Rm; best results for "a range of moderate Rm values, which is typically around Rm ≈ 1 ∼ 10", and the optimal range shifts as the number of sub-domains or the resolution changes. — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)
- The same Poisson problem at width 100: ELM error 1.864e+1 with Rm = 1 versus 4.673e-1 with the optimized Rm0 = 6, and at width 200, 5.633e+0 versus 1.242e-5, i.e. the scale alone moves the error by more than five orders. — [arXiv 2201.09989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09989)
- Dong and Yang's fix is an outer differential-evolution search over Rm (an extra cost the paper times separately). — [arXiv 2110.14121](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14121)
- RFM: Rm = 1 works with tanh; for sin/cos the sampling range should cover the solution's frequencies (Rm ≥ k for Helmholtz wavenumber k). — [ar5iv 2207.13380](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.13380)
- Later work proposes replacing blind random initialization with a preconditioning stage (RINN) because "the performance of PIELM is inherently sensitive to the initialization". — [arXiv 2506.17654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.17654)

**Domain decomposition and representation limits**
- A single PIELM cannot represent sharp-gradient or high-frequency solutions even with 7780 neurons; the distributed version with continuity constraints can, including a discontinuous case. — [arXiv 1907.03507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03507)
- locELM 2D Helmholtz: global ELM 410.6 s versus 4 sub-domains 33.6 s at similar accuracy (4.17e-5 versus 2.01e-5). — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)
- locELM and VarPro both rely on shallow networks; locELM states the method "is based on shallow feed-forward neural networks". — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)

**Time-dependent problems**
- Two strategies exist: treat time as another coordinate over the whole space-time slab, or block time marching, where each block's final state is the next block's initial condition. locELM notes that if the block is too large the nonlinear iterations can fail to converge. — [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)
- Chen, E, Luo, "The Random Feature Method for Time-dependent Problems", East Asian J. Appl. Math. 2023 (PR): with block time marching they "prove a lower error bound that shows an exponentially growing factor with respect to the number of blocks in time"; for the all-at-once space-time RFM they prove an upper bound with a sublinearly growing factor. Initial and boundary conditions are penalty terms. The complex-geometry 2D wave example reaches only 1e-2-level L2 errors relative to its own finest solution (e.g. 1.44e-2 at 20 collocation points per direction, 1.46e-2 at 350 features). — [arXiv 2304.06913](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06913); [journal](https://global-sci.org/intro/article_detail/eajam/21718.html)
- X-TFC's time-dependent examples show the cost of the all-at-once treatment: accuracy falls from 1e-13 (2D steady) to 5.8e-7 (1D heat, 196 neurons), 4.0e-4 (2D heat, 400 neurons, 13^3 points) and 3.0e-5 (nonlinear 3D+time, 340 neurons, 5^4 points). — [arXiv 2005.10632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.10632)

### Inferences
- The trade-off NLSQ versus Newton-LLSQ in locELM (1e-9 versus 1e-5) is the same phenomenon as Dong and Yang's "updated field versus increment" remark: how the linearized sub-problem is posed decides whether the least-squares accuracy of the linear case survives. Any compiled implementation should treat this as a design decision with a published four-order-of-magnitude consequence.
- Block time marching trades conditioning for error accumulation: an exactly satisfied initial condition per block (hard constraint in time) would remove the penalty-term mismatch at block interfaces, which is the mechanism Chen, E and Luo's lower bound rests on. No paper found tests whether a hard-constrained initial condition changes that exponential growth.
- The Rm sensitivity is the price of not training the hidden layer; VarPro removes most of it. So the two halves of the question are linked: exact last layer plus trained hidden weights is the published cure for the scale problem, at a cost nobody has measured in a compiled setting.

### Gaps
- No source found gives condition numbers of the least-squares matrix as a function of width and Rm together with the attained accuracy in a way that would allow choosing between QR, SVD and regularized normal equations on cost grounds.
- Primary texts of arXiv 2604.25502, 2602.10541 and 2506.17626 were not read; their descriptions come from search summaries.
- Picard iteration is not what the main ELM/RFM papers use; I found no ELM-family paper reporting a Picard-versus-Newton comparison.

## (B) Key papers on hard-constrained ansatz, reported gains versus penalty terms, and documented drawbacks

### Takeaway
The lineage is Lagaris (1998, box domains) → Berg and Nyström (2018, learned distance and extension functions) → TFC constrained expressions (2017–2021, rectangular domains, combined with ELM in X-TFC) → Sukumar and Srivastava (2022, R-functions and transfinite interpolation for Dirichlet, Neumann and Robin on complex 2D geometry). Gains over penalty terms are consistent but usually modest for gradient-trained PINNs (about one order of magnitude) and dramatic only when combined with a least-squares solve (X-TFC: 1e-13). The main documented drawback is unbounded second derivatives of distance functions at corners and on the medial axis, which restricts collocation use for second- and higher-order operators.

### Cited Findings

**Papers**
- Lagaris, Likas, Fotiadis, "Artificial neural networks for solving ordinary and partial differential equations", IEEE Trans. Neural Networks 9(5), 987-1000, 1998 (PR; DOI 10.1109/72.712178): trial solution = a term with no adjustable parameters that satisfies the initial/boundary conditions + a term built from a network that vanishes on the boundary, turning a constrained into an unconstrained problem; PDE examples on orthogonal box domains, Dirichlet and Neumann. — [arXiv physics/9705023](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/9705023)
- Berg and Nyström, "A unified deep artificial neural network approach to partial differential equations in complex geometries" (arXiv 2017, v2 Aug 2018): ansatz u = G(x) + D(x)·N(x) with G a smooth extension of the boundary data and D a smooth distance function, both pre-computed by low-capacity networks; advection and diffusion problems on complex 2D domains. — [arXiv 1711.06464](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.06464)
- Mortari, "The Theory of Connections: Connecting Points", Mathematics 5(4), 57 (2017) (PR): constrained expressions that satisfy linear constraints for any free function. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/5/4/57/htm)
- Leake and Mortari, "Deep Theory of Functional Connections: A New Method for Estimating the Solutions of PDEs", Mach. Learn. Knowl. Extr. 2(1), 37-55 (2020) (PR): the free function is a deep network trained on the PDE residual only. — [arXiv 1812.08625](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.08625.pdf); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7259480)
- Schiassi, Furfaro, Leake, De Florio, Johnston, Mortari, "Extreme Theory of Functional Connections", Neurocomputing 457 (2021) 334-356 (PR; DOI 10.1016/j.neucom.2021.06.015): TFC constrained expression with a single-layer ELM as free function, solved by (iterative) least squares. - Sukumar and Srivastava, "Exact imposition of boundary conditions with distance functions in physics-informed deep neural networks", CMAME 389 (2022) 114333 (PR): approximate distance functions from R-functions (R-equivalence joining) or mean value potentials; transfinite interpolation for inhomogeneous Dirichlet, Neumann and Robin data; collocation and Ritz formulations; second- and fourth-order problems. — [arXiv 2104.08426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08426)

- Dong and Ni, "A method for representing periodic functions and enforcing exactly periodic boundary conditions with deep neural networks", J. Comput. **Reported gains versus penalty terms**
- X-TFC, 2D steady linear PDE (Problem 1), 170 tanh neurons, 30x30 points, NumPy `lstsq`: training max error 3.808e-13, test max 5.054e-13; total run time 3.48 s of which the least-squares solve is 8.07 ms. Their comparison table: FEM 2e-8 (training) / 1.5e-5 (test), and three earlier neural methods at 5e-7, 3.2e-2 and 2.4e-4; "outperforms the other methods ... by 5 to 11 orders of magnitude". Intel Core i7-9700, 64 GB; PDEs in Python with autograd. — [arXiv 2005.10632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.10632)
- X-TFC, time-dependent: 1D heat, 196 neurons, 30x30 points, 3.21 s total (9.90 ms least squares), test max error 5.785e-7; 2D heat, 400 neurons, 13^3 points, 159.3 s total (55.4 ms solve), test max 4.025e-4; nonlinear 2D+time, 255 neurons, 8^3 points, 10 iterations, 32.19 s total (0.140 s solve), max 1.657e-8; nonlinear 3D+time, 340 neurons, 5^4 points, 10 iterations, 321.9 s total (0.229 s solve), test max 2.978e-5. — [arXiv 2005.10632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.10632)
- X-TFC's authors state the reported solve time "is the expected computation time if the partial derivative of the problem were computed analytically and explicitly programmed and/or if the problems were solved in a compiled language"; the full run times of "1 - 100 seconds" are autograd overhead. — [arXiv 2005.10632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.10632)
- X-TFC observes larger error where constraints are on the derivative than where they are on the value. — [arXiv 2005.10632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.10632)
- Sukumar and Srivastava, 1D bar problem, same hyperparameters: relative errors O(1e-5) for the standard PINN (equal loss weights) and O(1e-6) with the exact-BC ansatz; elsewhere O(1e-4) versus O(1e-5); for one case the 1–30–30–1 network "did not converge for standard PINN". They attribute the gap to boundary conditions being "poorly approximated" by the penalty form. — [arXiv 2104.08426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08426)
- hPINN: with soft constraints, "if the penalty coefficients are too large, the optimization problem becomes ill-conditioned"; the penalty and augmented Lagrangian variants reach the same objective as FDFD/FEM adjoint-based topology optimization, with designs "often simpler and smoother". The gain reported is for the PDE-as-constraint part, not for the BC ansatz in isolation. — [arXiv 2102.04626](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.04626.pdf)
- A 2024 preconditioning paper notes a distance-function ansatz "naturally adheres to the BC, eliminating loss imbalances" (search-result description). — [arXiv 2402.00531](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.00531)

**Documented drawbacks**
- Corner and medial-axis blow-up: "the Laplacians, ∇²φ_R and ∇²φ_M, blow up at the vertices of the square ... both are singular at the vertices of a polygon, and therefore very large in magnitude near any of its vertices", so in collocation "the contributions to the total loss from regions near the vertices can be very large". The remedy used is to keep interior collocation points inside [δ, 1−δ]² with δ = 0.01; modifying the ADF so the Laplacian stays bounded is left as future work. Ritz (first-derivative) formulations are unaffected. — [arXiv 2104.08426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08426)
- Exact distance functions cannot be used for second-order collocation in general because they have derivative discontinuities on the medial axis (example: distance to the unit circle, Laplacian unbounded at the origin); they are usable only when the medial axis lies outside the domain (annulus). — [arXiv 2104.08426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08426)
- Higher-order operators: a co-authored follow-up states the ADF method "suffers from an exploding Laplacian issue for losses that involve second- or higher-order derivatives. Thus its application for a range of problems with higher order PDEs is severely limited", and proposes a first-order reformulation (FO-PINN) partly to make exact BCs usable. — [arXiv 2210.14320](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.14320)
- Product-type ansatz in higher dimension: for a clamped 4D hypercube the naive product factor "scales as O(x^8) inside the biunit hypercube, and therefore the network parameters have to compensate for this highly nonlinear behavior during training". — [arXiv 2104.08426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08426)
- Mixed boundary types: with different conditions on different boundary segments "only R-equivalence is viable since this cannot be realized using the ADF based on mean value potential fields". Demonstrations are 2D (plus one 4D hypercube); complex 3D geometry is listed as future work. — [arXiv 2104.08426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08426)
- At a re-entrant corner, both ADF variants show "maximum errors near the singularity on the order of 20 percent". — [arXiv 2104.08426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08426)
- Training dynamics: Xie et al. (arXiv Dec 2025; preprint) derive an NTK for the ansatz u = A + B·N and show the boundary function B acts as a multiplicative spectral filter; "widely used boundary functions can inadvertently induce spectral collapse, leading to optimization stagnation despite exact boundary satisfaction"; residual-NTK magnitudes "span eight orders of magnitude across boundary function choices"; the second derivative B'' correlates negatively with effective rank; they recommend smooth families (tanh with α in [3, 5], rational functions). — [arXiv 2512.23295](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.23295v1)
- Geometry: X-TFC is demonstrated on rectangular domains and lists "non-rectangular domains" and a distributed version for sharp solutions and Navier–Stokes-type problems as future work. — [arXiv 2005.10632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.10632)
- A 2026 interface-problem paper repeats that defining distance functions near polygonal vertices "can lead to singular higher-order derivatives" (search-result description). — [alphaxiv 2604.08453](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.08453)

### Inferences
- The size of the benefit depends on what limits accuracy. In gradient-trained PINNs the ansatz buys roughly one order of magnitude (Sukumar: 1e-5 to 1e-6), because the optimizer, not the boundary penalty, is the binding limit. With a least-squares solve the constraint error is the binding limit, and removing it is worth far more: X-TFC reaches 1e-13 on a problem where penalty-based RFM needs careful row rescaling to do the same. Hard constraints and exact linear solves are therefore complements, not independent additions.
- Penalty-based ELM/RFM must balance PDE rows against boundary rows in one least-squares matrix (RFM's rescaling, Dong's λ weights); an ansatz removes those rows entirely and shrinks the system, which also removes one source of ill-conditioning. This is stated nowhere as a measured result; it follows from the formulations.
- For time-dependent problems on rectangular space-time domains (the 1D-in-space process models in question), the constraint surface is a box: the corner blow-up of R-function ADFs does not arise with simple polynomial or TFC-type factors, and the Lagaris/TFC form is exact. The documented drawbacks bite mainly for polygonal and curved 2D/3D geometry and for fourth-order operators.
- Xie et al.'s finding implies the choice of the vanishing factor is a conditioning decision, not only a correctness decision; a compiled engine that fixes one factor (e.g. t·(...) for the initial condition) should expect the factor's curvature to affect convergence of any gradient-type or Gauss-Newton hidden-layer update.

### Gaps
- Berg and Nyström's journal reference was not confirmed in this session (from memory: Neurocomputing 317 (2018) 28-41; verify).
- I did not extract numerical tables from Berrone et al. 2023 or from Dong and Ni 2021; only their stated conclusions are reported.
- Lagaris 1998 reports comparisons with FEM in the original paper; those numbers were not retrieved.
- No paper found quantifies the cost overhead of evaluating R-function ADFs and their derivatives inside the residual, on CPU, relative to the network evaluation.
- Robin and Neumann constraints: Sukumar and Srivastava give constructions and 2D examples, and X-TFC notes lower accuracy on derivative constraints, but I found no systematic accuracy study for Robin conditions with a hard ansatz on time-dependent problems.

## What remains unsolved or untested for either technique

### Takeaway
Each ingredient is established separately, and the pair "hard ansatz + exact linear solve with frozen random features" is established (X-TFC, 2021). What I found no published evidence for is the three-way combination (hard ansatz + exact last layer + Gauss-Newton or VarPro on trained hidden weights) on time-dependent nonlinear or inverse problems, and no error-versus-wall-clock study of any of these methods in compiled CPU code against tuned classical solvers.

### Cited Findings
- Inverse problems with frozen random features exist: Dong and Wang (arXiv Oct 2022; J. Comput. Phys. 2023 per the author's PDF title; PR) give three algorithms: NLLSQ (all unknowns jointly), VarPro-F1 (eliminate the PDE parameters, solve for network coefficients) and VarPro-F2 (eliminate network coefficients, solve a reduced problem in the PDE parameters only). Noise-free errors decrease exponentially toward machine accuracy. — [arXiv 2210.04338](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.04338); [JCP PDF](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sdong/PDF/InversePDE_JCP2023.pdf)
- Their PINN comparison (Q = 30x30 collocation, 100 measurement points): inverse Poisson, PINN/Adam parameter error 6.31E-3 in 134.5 s versus NLLSQ 1.66E-8 in 11.5 s (noise-free), and 5.53E-3 versus 9.72E-4 with 1 % noise; inverse advection, 1.18E-5 in 133.5 s versus 2.32E-10 in 29.6 s; inverse nonlinear Helmholtz, PINN parameter errors 7.08E-1 and 2.68E-1 in 3049.2 s versus 5.71E-9 and 3.05E-7 in 10.3 s. With noise the advantage shrinks to under one order (Poisson) and about two orders (Helmholtz: 4.34E-3). Scaling the measurement residual by λ_mea < 1 was needed for good noisy-data accuracy. Boundary conditions are penalty rows, not an ansatz. — [arXiv 2210.04338](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.04338)
- Bayesian PIELM (Liu et al., arXiv 2205.06948) treats unknown problem parameters as additional output-layer weights for noisy forward and inverse problems. — [arXiv 2205.06948](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.06948)
- X-TFC states it "can also handle data-driven solutions and data-driven discovery of parametric DEs" but the paper itself solves only exact (forward) problems, with ODE parameter discovery described as in progress. — [arXiv 2005.10632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.10632)
- Dong and Yang's VarPro paper (trained hidden layer, Gauss-Newton trust region) uses penalty-type boundary/initial conditions and block time marching, and reports no FEM or PINN comparison and no inverse problem. — [arXiv 2201.09989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09989)
- The 2025 LS-embedded hybrid trains hidden layers with Adam (first order), uses soft constraints, and is tested only on two 2D Helmholtz models; the authors list 3D and more complex wave scenarios as future work. — [arXiv 2504.16553](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16553v1)
- A targeted search for hard constraints + least-squares last layer + Gauss-Newton on hidden layers returned no paper combining all three; the closest hits each cover one or two pieces (LS last layer with Adam; KKT projection layers with Newton on the projection only; hard–soft adaptive PINNs with quasi-Newton but no LS layer). — [arXiv 2504.16553](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16553v1); [arXiv 2507.08124](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08124v2)
- A targeted search for 2024–2026 work pairing physics-informed ELM with variable projection for unknown PDE parameters found nothing newer than Dong and Wang; the one 2026 item (Burman, Larson, Larsson, Vallin) couples FEM with ELM surrogates and is not a VarPro method. — [arXiv 2602.14757](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14757v1)
- Time marching remains theoretically problematic for least-squares random-feature solvers: exponential error growth in the number of blocks is proved as a lower bound. — [arXiv 2304.06913](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06913)
- VarPro theory for deep networks and PINNs is recent and preliminary: Dus (Jan 2026) handles rank deficiency by regularization and demonstrates only toy problems. — [arXiv 2601.22897](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.22897)
- Timing evidence is confined to interpreted stacks: MATLAB 2017b (PIELM), TensorFlow/Keras + SciPy on a Mac i5 (Dong group), Python autograd + NumPy on an i7-9700 (X-TFC), TensorFlow CPU on an i5-10300H (Dus). — [arXiv 1907.03507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03507); [arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895); [arXiv 2005.10632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.10632); [arXiv 2601.22897](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.22897)
- Stiff ODE systems have been solved with random-projection networks and Gauss-Newton on output weights at cost "comparable" to ode45/ode15s in MATLAB, which is the nearest thing to a classical-solver wall-clock comparison for IVPs. — [arXiv 2108.01584](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.01584)

### Inferences
What is established (would not be new):
- Exact least-squares solve of the output layer with frozen random features, for linear and nonlinear, steady and time-dependent PDEs (PIELM, locELM, RFM).
- Hard-constrained ansatz for Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin and periodic conditions (Lagaris, TFC, Sukumar, Dong–Ni).
- The combination of the two with frozen features on box domains (X-TFC), including nonlinear problems by iterative least squares.
- VarPro with Gauss-Newton on hidden weights for PDEs with penalty constraints (Dong and Yang), and for network regression (Newman et al.).
- Inverse PDE problems with frozen random features, including projecting out either the parameters or the network coefficients (Dong and Wang).
- Alternating or embedded LS for the last layer with first-order training of hidden layers (Cyr et al.; Abedi et al.).

What I found no published instance of (candidates for novelty, subject to the search limits below):
- Hard-constrained ansatz + exact last-layer solve + Gauss-Newton/VarPro updates of hidden weights, in one method. Dong and Yang have the last two with penalties; X-TFC has the first two without hidden training.
- That three-way combination on time-dependent nonlinear problems with an exactly enforced initial condition per time block, and a test of whether exact block-interface conditions change the proved exponential error growth of block marching.
- Inverse problems with unknown parameters under a hard ansatz with an exact linear solve (Dong and Wang use penalty rows; X-TFC's inverse results are not in the 2021 paper). Note the structural point from Dong and Yang: once an unknown coefficient multiplies the solution, the problem stops being linear in the output weights, so either the parameters are projected out (VarPro-F1) or the weights are (VarPro-F2); which is better conditioned under noise is only partly explored.
- Error-versus-wall-clock curves in compiled CPU code with analytic or kernel-computed derivatives, against tuned classical solvers (method of lines + BDF, high-order FEM) and against second-order-trained PINNs. X-TFC explicitly predicts millisecond solves in a compiled language but does not measure them; every published timing is dominated by Python/MATLAB overhead, and every PINN baseline is Adam/L-BFGS.
- Behaviour on non-smooth or stiff solutions (steep fronts, kinks in forcing data): the published failures of global PIELM on sharp profiles and the absence of such cases in the Dong and X-TFC tables leave this open; domain decomposition is the only published remedy.
- Noise robustness: with 1 % noise the ELM advantage over PINN in Dong and Wang drops from six or more orders to between one and two; regularization of the exact solve under noisy data (the "regularization floor" question) is treated only by an ad hoc residual scaling λ_mea and by the Bayesian PIELM.

### Gaps
- Absence claims above rest on roughly a dozen targeted searches plus the reference lists of the papers read; they are "not found", not "proved absent". In particular I did not search SIAM J. Sci. Comput. or JCP tables of contents exhaustively for 2025–2026, and several 2025–2026 preprints (arXiv 2506.17626, 2506.17654, 2508.05921, 2602.10541, 2604.25502, 2604.05230, 2505.21404) were seen only as search-result summaries.
- I did not retrieve later X-TFC papers (stiff chemical kinetics, optimal control, inverse/data-driven discovery by Schiassi, De Florio and co-authors); these may contain inverse-problem results with a hard ansatz and an exact solve, which would narrow the third novelty candidate. This should be checked before claiming novelty there.
- I found no paper reporting ELM/RFM/VarPro results on counter-current or two-point-boundary-value process models, or on differential-algebraic systems, beyond the stiff-ODE benchmarks cited.
- No repository benchmarks were inspected; all numbers are from the papers' text and tables.
