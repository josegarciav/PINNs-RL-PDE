# Prior art narrows six PINN bets

Almost every method in the six bets is already published; what remains open is narrower and mostly a matter of measurement and combination: nobody has reported these methods in a compiled CPU engine, on small networks, at equal wall-clock time, with a per-equation reverse pass. Gauss-Newton, Levenberg-Marquardt, natural gradient and self-scaled quasi-Newton methods already beat Adam and L-BFGS by three to five orders of magnitude in error on smooth PDEs, but only on GPUs in float64 with generic automatic differentiation. Exact last-layer solves reach 1e-8 to 1e-12 in seconds, and their combination with a hard-constrained ansatz exists (X-TFC, 2021), yet the three-way combination with Gauss-Newton or variable projection on trained hidden weights was not found. Learned training controllers have thin, weakly-baselined evidence; the one unoccupied niche is control of damping, phase switching and restarts for second-order optimizers. No accepted benchmark plots error against wall-clock time, states a CPU timing, or has a small-network track, and classical solvers still win forward problems by orders of magnitude. The trunk-and-head form is DeepONet; recursive least squares on a physics-trained cached basis inside a control loop was not found, and surrogates in the loop often lose to the mechanistic model. Industrial readiness in dairy is decided by lifecycle requirements (shadow-mode validation on historian data, drift monitoring, change control, OPC UA integration), and the incumbent is plant-validated grey-box MPC, not a neural baseline. Every absence claim below is "not found in the searches the notes describe", and several 2026 preprints were read only as abstracts; those are flagged where they matter.

## Gauss-Newton already beats Adam by three to five orders of magnitude, on GPUs only

### The method line is settled and peer-reviewed at its core

The Gauss-Newton family for PINNs starts with energy natural gradient descent (ENGD; Müller and Zeinhofer, ICML 2023), which shows that a natural-gradient step in parameter space is a Newton step in function space projected onto the model's tangent space ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/muller23b.html)). It continues with KFAC for PINNs, which uses Taylor-mode AD to rewrite the differential operator as a weight-sharing forward network (Dangel, Müller and Zeinhofer, NeurIPS 2024) ([NeurIPS](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/3d27d607586984908900eaa8ce19c96c-Abstract-Conference.html)), ANaGRAM with cost min(P²S, S²P) (Schwencke and Furtlehner, ICLR 2025) ([ICLR](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/38b445e98823a02479582302ee3aa8e4-Abstract-Conference.html)), and matrix-free natural gradient with randomized Nyström or RPCholesky preconditioning (Bioli, Marcati and Sangalli, CMAME 464, 2026) ([arXiv 2505.11638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11638)). Levenberg-Marquardt is peer-reviewed through Bonfanti, Bruno and Cipriani (NeurIPS 2024), who show that for nonlinear PDEs the Hessian term does not vanish even at infinite width ([arXiv 2402.03864](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.03864)). The Hessian-based NysNewton-CG is Rathore et al. (ICML 2024 oral) ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/rathore24a.html)). The self-scaled quasi-Newton line (SSBFGS, SSBroyden) is peer-reviewed in Urbán, Stefanou and Pons (Journal of Computational Physics 523, 2025) ([arXiv 2405.04230](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.04230v2)), and SOAP in Wang, Bhartari, Li and Perdikaris (NeurIPS 2025) ([arXiv 2502.00604](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00604v2)).

The rest of the frontier is preprint: matrix-free Gauss-Newton for Navier-Stokes (Jnini, Vella and Zeinhofer, arXiv 2402.10680) ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2402.10680)), Woodbury ENGD and SPRING (Guzmán-Cordero et al., arXiv 2505.12149) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.12149v2)), dual natural gradient (D-NGD; Jnini and Vella, arXiv 2505.21404) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21404v2)), the SSBroyden study of Kiyani et al. (arXiv 2501.16371) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16371v6)), the April 2026 head-to-head by Jnini, Kiyani, Shukla, Urbán, Müller, Zeinhofer, Karniadakis and co-authors (arXiv 2604.05230) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05230v1)), the shallow-network LM study on a CPU (Shahab et al., arXiv 2602.08515) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.08515)), and the doubly-sketched Gauss-Newton DSGNAR (Webb, Jerad and Cartis, arXiv 2607.02194, July 2026) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02194)). The April 2026 paper joins the natural-gradient and quasi-Newton camps and recommends natural-gradient methods as a first choice for elliptic and parabolic PDEs, which is the closest thing to a consensus statement. A matrix-free Gauss-Newton or LM optimizer for PINNs is therefore **not new as a method**: Jnini et al. (2024), the Hessian-free baseline in the KFAC paper, the PCG variant of D-NGD and Bioli et al. all do it with JVP and VJP products.

### The strongest numbers, and what they were measured on

ENGD set the pattern on a 257-weight network in float64: on 2D Poisson, **median relative L2 2.4e-7 for ENGD against 1.1e-3 for Adam and 4.4e-4 for BFGS, in 43 s against about one hour for Adam**; on a 1D heat equation, 6.3e-6 against 1.0e-3 for Adam, in under three minutes against two hours ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.13163)). Bioli et al. give the cleanest peer-reviewed error-and-seconds table (RTX 4090, 8,641 parameters, H1 error): on 3+1D heat, Nyström-preconditioned NGD reaches 1.89e-6 in 106 s, SSBroyden 2.39e-6 in 477 s, L-BFGS 3.91e-4 in 868 s and Adam 1.87e-3 in 1,078 s; on Kovasznay flow SSBroyden is both faster and more accurate than every natural-gradient variant (5.45e-7 in 329 s) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11638v6)). In the April 2026 preprint on an H100, natural gradient reaches 5.8e-7 on unsteady viscous Burgers in 75 s where Adam plus SSBroyden needs 612 s for 5.3e-7, and 150 s on Stokes where SOAP plus SSBFGS needs 3,600 s; that is an 8x and a 24x advantage at matched error ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05230v1)). Kiyani et al. report Burgers at **1.62e-8 in 2,812 s** with 3,021 parameters and Allen-Cahn at 9.43e-7 in 2,000 s with 2,019 parameters (preprint, GPU, float64) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16371v6)). SOAP's peer-reviewed numbers on the PirateNet architecture are more modest but cover harder problems: Burgers 4.03e-5, Allen-Cahn 3.48e-6, KdV 3.40e-4, Kuramoto-Sivashinsky 3.51e-2, and first PINN results at Reynolds numbers up to 10,000, at about twice the training time of the baseline ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00604v2)).

Three caveats govern how these numbers can be used. Cross-paper comparisons are not controlled: SOAP on a plain MLP reaches only 4.2e-4 on Helmholtz and 1.7e-2 on viscous Burgers in the April 2026 study, far from its PirateNet figures ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05230v1)). DSGNAR's headline results (Burgers 4.75e-7 in 9.8 s in single precision, 7.97e-14 in 346 s in double, 5D Poisson 3.03e-16) come from a July 2026 preprint whose speed ratios compare its own H100 times with times others published on other hardware; **treat them as unverified claims** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02194)). And the only CPU evidence is the MATLAB study by Shahab et al. on an Intel Core i5-10400 with 751 to 1,552 parameters: Burgers at 2.0e-4 against 2.2e-2 for BFGS, Schrödinger at 2.1e-5 against 2.7e-3 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.08515)). The notes record two different timings for those runs (1,962 and 2,281 for Burgers; 8,856 and 10,303 for Schrödinger, from different paper versions, with unlabeled table units), so the times should be re-read from the PDF before being quoted.

### Dense solves are cheap at the scale of interest; conditioning is the documented risk

Most high-accuracy results form a matrix: the P x P Gramian (ENGD, LM), the N x N kernel (Woodbury ENGD, dense D-NGD, the natural gradient of the April 2026 study), a dense inverse Hessian (the BFGS family) or a sketched Jacobian (DSGNAR). The Woodbury authors conclude that the sample-space solve "is the right way to implement second-order methods such as ENGD", cutting cost from O(P³) to O(N²P) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.12149v2)). Matrix-free variants pay for the Gramian's conditioning: Bioli et al. find unpreconditioned matrix-free NGD clearly less accurate than the preconditioned variants ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11638v6)), and in the matrix-free Navier-Stokes experiment accuracy is "primarily constrained" by the CG tolerance, with soft constraints costing about an order of magnitude against hard ones ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2402.10680)). For networks of about 1,000 parameters and 1,000 to 10,000 residual rows, a dense Jacobian is 8 to 80 MB and a Cholesky factorization is trivial, so a matrix-free solver has to earn its place by skipping Jacobian assembly; none of the papers measures that crossover.

The other failure modes are equally well documented. Second-order methods are local refiners: ENGD "occasionally failed to train" on the heat equation (worst seed 5.6e-1) and did not converge stand-alone on Kovasznay; Rathore et al. and Urbán et al. both run Adam first because Newton-type and quasi-Newton methods are attracted to saddles ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.13163); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01868v1)). Memory bites early: ENGD runs out above about 10,000 parameters, and a dense SSBFGS Hessian at 33,667 parameters holds about 9 GB ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15603v1); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05230v1)). Float64 is called "crucial", and Wang et al. exclude natural-gradient methods from their comparison because they need full batches, double precision and small networks ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00604v2)). Curvature does not help on shocks: on inviscid Burgers and Euler, natural gradient is slower and no more accurate than SSBroyden, and "optimization alone is not sufficient for discontinuous problems" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05230v1)). Damping remains hand-set everywhere; the Woodbury ENGD authors say their hyperparameters "are not yet adaptive".

The accepted explanation for why first-order methods stall is conditioning inherited from the operator. De Ryck et al. (ICLR 2024) show gradient descent needs O(κ ln(1/ε)) steps with κ bounded below by the conditioning of the Hermitian square of the PDE operator composed with the tangent kernel, growing like K⁴ for Poisson with maximum frequency K ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.05801)). Rathore et al. add that conditioning worsens as collocation points are added, which predicts more CG iterations per step at larger N for an unpreconditioned solver; no paper tests that directly ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01868v1)).

### Verdict for bet 1

Not new: that Gauss-Newton and LM beat Adam and L-BFGS on smooth PDEs with small networks, the sample-space formulation, matrix-free CG with a preconditioner, the pairing with Taylor mode, and equal-wall-clock comparisons on GPUs (the KFAC, Woodbury and D-NGD papers use fixed time budgets). Open, and specific to a compiled per-equation engine: dedicated compiled kernels for J v and Jᵀ w of the PDE residual with their cost measured relative to one gradient (Jnini et al. claim "constant overhead" without timings); the dense-versus-matrix-free crossover in (P, N) on a CPU, including networks under 1,000 parameters; equal-wall-clock curves against Adam and L-BFGS on the same engine and machine, which removes the framework and hardware confound present in every cross-paper comparison; CG iteration counts per outer step as a function of N and damping; LM on inverse problems, tested once; and single-precision behaviour of CG-based steps. Two results must be positioned against, ideally by reproducing their Burgers setups: DSGNAR's seconds-scale claim and Shahab et al.'s CPU LM. The hypothesis that a CPU with the working set in cache competes in this regime has indirect support only: the H100 roofline analysis finds the small 1D Burgers kernel memory-bound ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05230v1)).

## An exact last layer reaches 1e-10 in seconds, and a hard ansatz is what unlocks it

### Bet 2: solving the linear output layer exactly

The lineage is PIELM (Dwivedi and Srinivasan, arXiv 2019, journal version in Neurocomputing 2020 not confirmed in the notes) ([arXiv 1907.03507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03507)), locELM (Dong and Li, CMAME 387, 2021) ([arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)), the random feature method (Chen, Chi, E and Yang, Journal of Machine Learning 2022) ([arXiv 2207.13380](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13380)), and variable projection for PDEs (Dong and Yang, CMAME 398, 2022) ([arXiv 2201.09989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09989)), with general-network VarPro in Newman et al. (SIAM Journal on Mathematics of Data Science, 2021) ([arXiv 2007.13171](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.13171)). All are peer-reviewed.

The locELM tables are the strongest evidence. On 1D Helmholtz, **locELM reaches a maximum error of 1.56e-9 in 1.1 s where PINN with Adam reaches 1.06e-3 in 508 s and with L-BFGS 1.98e-4 in 1,036 s**. On viscous Burgers, the nonlinear least-squares variant reaches 1.85e-8 in 27.6 s against 4.57e-2 in 1,798 s for a deep Galerkin network with Adam, and against a FEniCS finite-element best case of 4.74e-6 in 92.6 s. On 1D diffusion it reaches 8.97e-8 in 11.3 s against FEM's 4.24e-6 in 179 s ([arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)). The same group later restated the result less favourably: locELM "can barely outperform" cubic Lagrange elements and "cannot outcompete high-order FEM with element degrees 4 or larger", though with an optimized random-feature scale they claim ELM far outperforms second-order FEM except at very small problem sizes ([arXiv 2110.14121](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.14121)). Variable projection on trained hidden weights turns a width-100 network from useless into accurate on 2D Poisson (6.0e-7 against ELM's 1.5e+1 at 15 x 15 points) but costs 25 to 250 times more time than ELM in the authors' table (78 s against 0.32 s on advection) ([arXiv 2201.09989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09989)). That penalty comes from a generic SciPy trust-region solver with dense TensorFlow Jacobians; it is an implementation-bound number. The random feature method reports errors down to about 1e-11 to 1e-13 on Helmholtz, Poisson and Stokes without timings, but the notes took those figures from an automated extraction and flag an internal inconsistency in that paper's elasticity comparisons, so they are approximate ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.13380)).

Four limits are documented. The least-squares matrix is ill-conditioned, and normal equations "lead to severe ill-conditioning and significantly lower accuracy", so the Dong group uses QR or SVD routines ([arXiv 2110.14121](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.14121)). Accuracy is acutely sensitive to the random-feature scale: the same Poisson problem at width 200 gives 5.6e+0 with scale 1 and 1.2e-5 with scale 6 ([arXiv 2201.09989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09989)). A single global network fails on sharp or high-frequency solutions even with 7,780 neurons, and domain decomposition is the only published remedy ([arXiv 1907.03507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03507)). And block time marching carries a proved lower bound with an error factor growing exponentially in the number of blocks (Chen, E and Luo, East Asian Journal on Applied Mathematics, 2023) ([arXiv 2304.06913](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.06913)). For nonlinear PDEs, how the linearized sub-problem is posed is worth four orders of magnitude: locELM's nonlinear least squares reaches 1.45e-9 on nonlinear Helmholtz where its Newton-plus-linear-solve variant reaches 1.28e-5 ([arXiv 2012.02895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.02895)). Inverse problems with frozen features exist: Dong and Wang (Journal of Computational Physics, 2023) recover parameters to 1.66e-8 on inverse Poisson in 11.5 s against 6.31e-3 in 134.5 s for a PINN, but with 1 % noise the advantage shrinks to under one order of magnitude ([arXiv 2210.04338](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.04338)).

One reading caution applies to the whole literature: every PINN baseline in these tables is Adam or L-BFGS in TensorFlow, never a Gauss-Newton or natural-gradient trainer, so the published five-to-nine-order gaps overstate the advantage over a well-trained PINN. Every FEM comparison favouring ELM comes from one group, on one Mac, mostly on smooth manufactured solutions.

### Bet 3: hard-constrained ansatz

The lineage runs from Lagaris, Likas and Fotiadis (IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks, 1998) ([arXiv physics/9705023](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/9705023)) through Berg and Nyström's learned distance functions (2018) ([arXiv 1711.06464](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.06464)), the theory of functional connections (Mortari, Mathematics 2017; Leake and Mortari, 2020) ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/5/4/57/htm)), X-TFC (Schiassi et al., Neurocomputing 457, 2021) ([arXiv 2005.10632](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.10632)), exact periodic layers (Dong and Ni, Journal of Computational Physics 435, 2021) ([arXiv 2007.07442](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.07442)), hard-constraint inverse design (Lu et al., SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing, 2021) ([arXiv 2102.04626](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.04626.pdf)), and R-function distance fields with transfinite interpolation for Dirichlet, Neumann and Robin conditions (Sukumar and Srivastava, CMAME 389, 2022) ([arXiv 2104.08426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08426)).

The gain depends on what limits accuracy. In gradient-trained PINNs the ansatz buys about one order of magnitude: Sukumar and Srivastava report O(1e-5) for the standard PINN against O(1e-6) with exact boundary conditions ([arXiv 2104.08426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08426)), and Berrone et al. (Heliyon, 2023) find exact enforcement best among four methods ([arXiv 2210.14795](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.14795)). With a least-squares solve the constraint error becomes the binding limit and removing it is worth far more: **X-TFC reaches a maximum error of 5e-13 on a 2D steady problem with 170 neurons in 3.48 s total, of which the least-squares solve is 8 ms**; on 1D heat it reaches 5.8e-7 in 3.2 s. Its authors say the solve time "is the expected computation time if the partial derivative of the problem were computed analytically and explicitly programmed and/or if the problems were solved in a compiled language"; the remaining seconds are autograd overhead ([arXiv 2005.10632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.10632)). That sentence is a published prediction that a compiled engine has not yet been measured against.

The main documented drawback is geometric. Laplacians of R-function distance fields "blow up at the vertices" of polygons, exact distance functions are unusable for second-order collocation when the medial axis lies inside the domain, and a follow-up states the method's use for higher-order PDEs "is severely limited" ([arXiv 2104.08426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08426); [arXiv 2210.14320](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.14320)). On rectangular space-time domains, the case of 1D-in-space process models, simple polynomial or TFC-type factors avoid the corner problem. A second caution is about training dynamics: a December 2025 preprint (Xie et al.) derives a tangent kernel for the ansatz u = A + B·N and shows the vanishing factor B acts as a spectral filter, with residual-kernel magnitudes spanning eight orders of magnitude across choices of B ([arXiv 2512.23295](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.23295v1)). The choice of factor is a conditioning decision, and it will affect any Gauss-Newton update of hidden weights.

### Verdict for bets 2 and 3

Established: exact output-layer solves with frozen random features for linear and nonlinear, steady and time-dependent PDEs; hard ansatz for all common boundary types; the pair of them on box domains (X-TFC); VarPro with Gauss-Newton on hidden weights under penalty constraints (Dong and Yang); inverse problems with frozen features (Dong and Wang); least squares for the last layer with first-order training of the rest (Cyr et al., MSML 2020; Abedi et al., 2025 preprint) ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v107/cyr20a.html); [arXiv 2504.16553](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16553v1)). Not found: the three-way combination of hard ansatz, exact last layer and Gauss-Newton or VarPro on trained hidden weights; that combination on time-dependent nonlinear problems with an exactly enforced initial condition per time block, including a test of whether exact block interfaces change the proved exponential error growth; inverse problems under a hard ansatz with an exact solve; and error-versus-wall-clock curves in compiled CPU code against tuned classical solvers and second-order-trained PINNs. The notes rest these absences on about a dozen searches and flag that later X-TFC papers on inverse and data-driven discovery were not retrieved, which could narrow the inverse-problem claim.

## Learned training controllers lack fair evidence; damping control is the unoccupied niche

The hand-designed baselines a controller must beat are strong, cheap and unstable in rank. For collocation, Wu et al. (CMAME 403, 2023; more than 6,000 runs) report RAD at 0.02 % relative error on Burgers against 13.3 % for fixed random points, but **uniform resampling alone already reaches 1.69 %**, and the comparison is at equal residual points, not equal compute ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.10289)). In R3 (Daw et al., ICML 2023), fresh uniform samples every iteration tie the best adaptive methods on Allen-Cahn (0.77 % against 0.83 % for R3), while RAD, "always the best" in Wu et al., reaches 64 % on convection with β = 50 ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.02338)). For loss weights, ReLoBRaLo (Bischof and Kraus, CMAME 2025) wins by one to two orders of magnitude on some PDEs and loses to a grid-searched fixed-weight baseline on others, at a cost of +0.6 s per 1,000 steps on Burgers ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.09813)). ConFIG (Liu, Chu and Thuerey, ICLR 2025 spotlight) is one of the few weighting papers with an equal wall-clock comparison ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.11104v3)). PINNacle finds reweighting and resampling help "in some cases" at 1.5 to 3 times the runtime, for example 764 s for learning-rate annealing against 284 s for the vanilla PINN on Burgers ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.08827)).

The learned side is thin. RL-based collocation selection exists only as preprints: RL-PINNs (Song, arXiv 2504.12949, single author) uses a DQN whose reward is function variation, reports Burgers at 0.053 against 0.147 for RAD, but with one run per cell, baselines at 13 to 40 % error, unequal point counts and an agent trained inside each run ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.12949v1)). A May 2026 preprint by Kang and Zhang gives no numbers on its abstract page ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09707)). The notes also record that the only software artefact for RL-driven sampling is the pinnrl package on PyPI, which is software without a controlled comparison ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pinnrl/0.1.0/)). No bandit formulation of collocation selection or loss weighting was found, and no RL agent that sets loss weights.

Learned optimizers transfer weakly. Bihlo (JMLR 25, 2024) meta-trains a 2,115-parameter optimizer that beats Adam by more than 10x on linear advection, but Adam is the only baseline, there are no repeated seeds, and the cross-equation test (advection to Burgers) shows early gains, then "substantially higher oscillations" and stagnation after about 600 epochs; the author calls transfer "proof-of-concept" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.07127)). Psaros, Kawaguchi and Karniadakis (Journal of Computational Physics 458, 2022) meta-learn loss functions within single PDE families ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.05544)). Metamizer (Wandel, Schulz and Klein, ICLR 2025) claims generalization to unseen PDEs but operates on grids, not PINNs ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19746)). None charges meta-training cost to the comparison, and none compares against L-BFGS or second-order methods.

For damping, prior art outside PINNs sizes the prize honestly. Ruvolo, Fasel and Movellan (NeurIPS 2008) learn an LM damping controller that gains **6 % in loss reduction on classical test problems and 20 % on a vision task once the comparison is restricted to LM's own action set**; the 2.66-fold gain came from a random-restart action, and the learned policy rediscovers the standard heuristic ([NeurIPS 2008](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2008/file/9a96876e2f8f3dc4f3cf45f02c61c0c1-Paper.pdf)). Belder, Vivanti and Tal (2023 preprint) learn damping for bundle adjustment with soft actor-critic and cut iterations from 20 to 4, but against a multiply-or-halve rule in Python; a constant damping already cut 20 to 12 ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.13270)). Neither tests Nielsen's rule, a gain-ratio trust region or Transtrum and Sethna's delayed gratification ([arXiv 1201.5885](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1201.5885)).

The background rate of overclaiming is high. McGreivy and Hakim (Nature Machine Intelligence 6, 2024) find 79 % (60 of 76) of ML-for-PDE articles claiming superiority compare against a weak baseline ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.07218v1)), and in multi-task learning, the closest analogue to loss balancing, Kurin et al. (NeurIPS 2022) show plain summed losses match or beat specialized optimizers ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04122)). The trend since 2024 treats loss imbalance as a conditioning problem solved by curvature: Urbán et al. find optimizer choice matters more than loss modifications. A weighting controller attached to a second-order optimizer may have little left to fix.

**Verdict for bet 4.** A learned controller is a credible bet only in a narrow form. No controller meta-trained across equations and tested on held-out operators with multi-seed, equal-compute reporting was found, and no learned control of damping, Adam-to-quasi-Newton switching, curvature refresh or restarts for second-order PINN optimizers, although those decisions are documented as hand-tuned and consequential (Rathore et al. select the switch point per problem from three values, and L-BFGS often stops early when its line search fails) ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2402.01868)). The lowest-risk design is a bandit over known rules, which cannot do much worse than its best arm. The protocol the critiques imply is error against wall-clock and against residual and Jacobian-vector-product evaluations, controller inference charged, meta-training cost reported with a break-even number of solves, at least five seeds, baselines tuned with the same budget, and a held-out equation set fixed in advance. A well-run null result is publishable. The compiled engine's contribution here is indirect: it makes that protocol cheap enough to run. The notes did not read the full April 2026 curvature paper, so whether it pre-empts damping or switching schedules is unknown.

## No benchmark plots error against wall-clock, and every published timing is on a GPU

### The suites and what they measure

PINNacle (Hao et al., NeurIPS 2024 Datasets and Benchmarks) is the only PINN-method benchmark in the strict sense: 22 tasks, a 5 x 100 MLP, 20,000 Adam epochs, three seeds, and a runtime table in seconds without a GPU model in the text read. Its headline is that the vanilla PINN solves 10 of 22 tasks at a 10 % error threshold, and every method fails on the chaotic Kuramoto-Sivashinsky task ([arXiv 2306.08827](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.08827)). The de facto accuracy leaderboard is the jaxpi and PirateNets line (Expert's Guide, 2023 preprint; PirateNets, JMLR 25, 2024), which reports final error from per-problem tuned pipelines with 256-wide networks and no wall-clock time or GPU model ([arXiv 2308.08468](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.08468); [JMLR](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume25/24-0313/24-0313.pdf)). The same Burgers task differs by more than two orders of magnitude between the two protocols (1.33e-2 in PINNacle, 8.20e-5 for PirateNet), so the protocol, not the method, dominates the number. PDEBench (NeurIPS 2022) is a data-driven surrogate benchmark whose PINN baseline is deliberately plain and whose timing table compares FNO inference with simulation ([arXiv 2210.07182](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.07182v7)); The Well, CFDBench and PDEArena were seen only through abstracts or search summaries. The only cross-framework timing table is from the jinns authors (preprint, laptop NVIDIA T600): Burgers in 445 s for jinns, 723 s for DeepXDE-JAX, 671 s for DeepXDE-PyTorch, 1,977 s for PINA and 646 s for Modulus ([arXiv 2412.14132](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.14132v1)). No official step-time benchmark was found for DeepXDE, PhysicsNeMo, NeuralPDE.jl or PINA, and no CPU cross-framework comparison in any paper.

### What the critiques demand

McGreivy and Hakim excluded PINNs from their main review "because it is already well-known that standard numerical methods outperform PINNs for forward problems", found that **only 12 % (7 of 60) of sampled fluid PINN articles report efficiency against a standard solver anywhere in the text**, and set two rules: compare at equal accuracy or equal runtime, against an efficient numerical method ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.07218)). Grossmann et al. (IMA Journal of Applied Mathematics 89, 2024) found FEM five to six orders of magnitude faster than PINNs on Allen-Cahn and one to three orders faster on Poisson, with PINNs ahead only in evaluation time ([arXiv 2302.04107](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.04107)). Chuang and Barba report six hours on a V100 for a PINN against under ten seconds for a CFD code at 1e-2 error on Taylor-Green, while conceding their PINN configuration "might not be optimal" ([arXiv 2306.00230](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.00230)).

**Verdict for bet 5.** No accepted benchmark provides error-versus-wall-clock curves, CPU timings, a small-network track, or inverse problems scored on identifiability and calibration; the only inverse tasks in a suite are PINNacle's two noise-free coefficient recoveries scored by L2 error. A benchmark with all four would be new. It must carry a justified classical solver's own error-versus-time curve, at least one external PINN framework run on the same CPU, declared precision, five or more seeds, full optimization time including warm-up and all windows, and the published anchors overlaid with their own hardware labels. The honest framing is "fastest PINN at a given error on a CPU, with the classical gap shown". Equations with agreed definitions are Burgers, Allen-Cahn, Raissi's Schrödinger and the PINNacle tasks; for heat, Cahn-Hilliard and forward KdV the team defines the problem and cannot claim a record. The notes did not search for Bayesian PINN benchmarks with calibration scoring, so "no incumbent" for the inverse track rests on the suites reviewed.

## The trunk-and-head form is DeepONet; the loop-oriented use is what remains open

### The representation and its online update are both prior art

The form u(x, t; z) = Σ c_j(z) φ_j(x, t) is the DeepONet output layer. Physics-informed DeepONet (Wang, Wang and Perdikaris, Science Advances 2021) predicts a full Burgers field in about 10 ms on a V100 at about 1.4 % relative error after 7 to 9 hours of training ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.10974)). POD-DeepONet (Lu et al., CMAME 2022) replaces the trunk with a precomputed basis ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.05512v1)), and two-step training (Lee and Shin) fits the trunk first and the coefficients by least squares ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.01020v1)). Hypernetwork variants generalize by modulating inner weights: HyperPINN (NeurIPS 2021 workshop) evaluates a 393-parameter generated network in 86 microseconds on a V100 ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2111.01008)), and Hyper-LR-PINN (Cho et al., NeurIPS 2023) reaches 3.9 % relative error on convection with β = 30 where MAML and Reptile stay above 55 % ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.09528)). Hypernetworks cache in the opposite direction, fixing z and reusing the generated network over (x, t); for a loop where z changes every step and query points are fixed, the cached trunk is the cheaper side. Its price is a fixed subspace, and the β = 30 convection case is the reference test any fixed-basis design should face. The notes found no sourced material on FiLM conditioning and did not search the certified reduced-basis literature, the classical precedent for an offline basis with a small online solve.

Online update of a linear head on a learned basis is established outside PDE surrogates. Neural-Fly (O'Connell et al., Science Robotics 2022) adapts 12 coefficients on a meta-learned basis at 50 Hz on a Raspberry Pi 4, with **about 3.5 ms for inference and 3.0 ms for the adaptation update**, and proves exponential convergence of the tracking error to a ball without assuming persistent excitation ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2205.06908)). Korda and Mezić (Automatica 2018) run Koopman MPC of a KdV equation with 385 lifting functions at 0.28 ms per control move on a laptop CPU ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1611.03537)). Recursive Koopman preprints warn that RLS-based methods "lack comprehensive convergence and complexity analyses" and have "severe numerical vulnerabilities that are frequently overlooked" ([arXiv 2509.08241](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08241v1); [arXiv 2606.15317](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15317)). Adaptive POD models updated recursively inside MPC for distributed process systems date to the Armaou group around 2011 ([CDC-ECC 2011](https://skoge.folk.ntnu.no/prost/proceedings/cdc-ecc-2011/data/papers/1977.pdf)). The principled alternative that does not fix the subspace is Neural Galerkin (Bruna, Peherstorfer and Vanden-Eijnden, Journal of Computational Physics 2024), which pays a linear solve with an ill-conditioned matrix every step and reports no runtimes in the text read ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.01360v4)).

### Surrogates in the loop frequently lose

The evidence on physics-informed world models is mixed and contains a direct warning. PINC matches an ODE model inside NMPC on a four-tank system, with total simulation time 10.85 s against 14.15 s, and has no mechanism for model error or disturbance rejection ([arXiv 2104.02556](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.02556)). The Waterloo plug-flow-reactor benchmark (Elorza Casas, Ricardez-Sandoval and Pulsipher, preprint) is the negative result: the PINN surrogate evaluates slightly faster than direct simulation (0.42 ms against 0.50 ms), yet **the NMPC step takes 0.402 s with the best PINN embedding against 0.120 s with the mechanistic model, and on the hardest benchmark about 2,000 s against 16 s**. The authors conclude "the mechanistic NMPC problem is significantly more performant on all benchmarks" and attribute the loss to the modelling language's AD being more efficient on the mechanistic model than PyTorch's on the surrogate ([arXiv 2501.06335](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.06335)). A July 2026 laser-processing preprint shows the complementary point: a surrogate forward pass of 0.42 ms on a CPU sits inside an MPC step of 41 ms dominated by IPOPT, and "two surrogates with near-identical offline error behave drastically differently in closed loop" ([arXiv 2607.13289](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.13289)).

For reinforcement learning, Liu and Wang (Proceedings of the Royal Society A, 2021) reach a target return on Kuramoto-Sivashinsky with about 15 % of the real-environment steps of model-free TD3, but switch to real-environment fine-tuning because "model bias becomes the bottleneck" ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10311898)). Surrogate-only agents fall short: on Rayleigh-Bénard convection a surrogate-only policy reaches a Nusselt number of 2.97 against 2.74 for the solver-trained agent, with rollouts 25.6 times faster than the simulation (March 2026 preprint) ([arXiv 2603.28074](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28074v1)); on Kuramoto-Sivashinsky a model-based controller reaches 63 % reduction against 73 % for SAC ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.07160)). No source reports environment throughput in steps per second, including PDE Control Gym, HydroGym and FluidGym. Rollout stability is addressed by training tricks (the pushforward trick, PDE-Refiner) with formal guarantees coming almost only from the Koopman and adaptive-control side ([arXiv 2202.03376](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.03376); [ar5iv 2308.05732](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.05732)).

On deployment hardware, the notes found no PINN running on a PLC and no hardware-in-the-loop PINN controller. Reported latencies are milliseconds on single-board computers and microseconds only on FPGAs or GPUs. The loop-period figures available (PLC scan cycles of 1 to 10 ms, process loops of 100 ms to 1 s) come from a security preprint and need a primary reference ([arXiv 2608.16769](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.16769)). At those periods a single surrogate evaluation is never the constraint; the optimizer's iterations and RL training volume are.

**Verdict for bet 6.** Do not claim the representation, least-squares coefficients on a fixed learned basis, online last-layer adaptation, or surrogates inside MPC and Dyna-style RL. Not found, and each conditional on about 45 searches that did not cover IFAC-PapersOnLine or the Journal of Process Control by venue: RLS on the coefficients of a physics-trained space-time basis whose update rows mix sensor readings and PDE-residual rows from cached trunk derivatives; closed-form gradients of the field with respect to z and of the cost with respect to actions through the linear head, which addresses exactly the AD cost the Waterloo benchmark isolates; a contractivity certificate for chained windows computed in coefficient space; measured batch-1 CPU latency and jitter for a cached trunk as a compiled kernel; a loop-throughput benchmark reported alongside closed-loop return or integral absolute error; and a surrogate-only policy that matches a solver-trained one on a process PDE, with the comparison run. The literature's risks carry over directly: fixed-subspace failure on transport fronts such as those in evaporators and dryers, speed that does not reach the loop when the optimizer dominates, RLS wind-up without persistent excitation, and equal field error not implying equal closed-loop behaviour. The acceptance test is closed-loop, not field L2.

## Taylor mode buys about 2x at second order; the generated reverse pass is the open claim

Taylor-mode AD is classical (Griewank and Walther, *Evaluating Derivatives*, chapter 13) and entered JAX through Bettencourt, Johnson and Duvenaud (NeurIPS 2019 workshop) ([ML Anthology](https://mlanthology.org/neuripsw/2019/bettencourt2019neuripsw-taylormode)). The honest reference for its speed comes from Dangel, Siebert, Zeinhofer and Walther (NeurIPS 2025): on a wide MLP on a GPU, **vanilla JAX Taylor mode is 50 % slower than nested backpropagation for the Laplacian**, and only the collapsed form, which propagates the sum of the highest coefficients, is about twice as fast as nesting with 40 to 50 % of the memory; the stochastic biharmonic gains up to 9x ([arXiv 2505.13644](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)). STDE (Shi et al., NeurIPS 2024) reports about 2x over repeated backward mode on low-dimensional high-order operators such as KdV; its ">1000x" headline is against an unoptimized PyTorch baseline in very high dimension ([arXiv 2412.00088](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.00088)). Separable PINNs' 62x comes from a separable architecture (Cho et al., NeurIPS 2023) ([arXiv 2306.15969](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15969)). n-TangentProp (Chickering, 2024 preprint) reports over 2.5x at three derivatives and over 7x at five on a 3 x 24 network; its 25x and 100x figures at seven and nine derivatives are projections, not measurements ([arXiv 2412.09752](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09752v1)). Large second-order speed-up claims therefore have to be attributed to something other than the AD mode: compilation, fusion, removed framework overhead, or the small-network CPU regime.

The closest prior art to a per-equation engine is Cao, Lu and Zhang (arXiv 2606.29702, June 2026, preprint): residual-specific partial-jet propagation through specialized layerwise kernels, written in PyTorch and JAX by a programming agent and verified against nested AD. On an RTX 5080 in float32 they report end-to-end training speed-ups over nested AD of 1.22x on Burgers, 2.30x on Navier-Stokes and 3.58x on Kuramoto-Sivashinsky in PyTorch, and 2.28x, 2.27x and 3.01x in JAX, where jit-compiled Taylor mode alone gives 1.31x to 1.76x. Their scope limit is explicit: "parameter gradients for training are still computed by reverse-mode differentiation of the scalar loss; ED replaces only the coordinate-derivative evaluation" ([arXiv 2606.29702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29702)). A July 2026 preprint claims a 40 to 50 times cheaper training step from forward second-order jets plus one-time graph compilation; the notes verified only the abstract, so its decomposition is unverified ([arXiv 2607.28733](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28733)).

Generic compilers do not close the gap. Dangel et al. state "JAX's jit compiler is unable to apply the graph rewrites we propose", that `torch.compile` leaves runtime "roughly identical", and that their own Taylor arithmetic sticks to Faà di Bruno and does not implement the efficient Griewank-Walther recurrences ([arXiv 2505.13644](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13644v2)). `torch.compile` with AOTAutograd rejects double backward outright ([PyTorch forum](https://discuss.pytorch.org/t/double-backward-in-torch2-0/175454)). The nearest CPU-first evidence is SimpleChains.jl's claim of more than 5x over PyTorch on small networks, which is the project's own blog and covers first-order training, not PINN residuals ([Julia blog](https://www.julialang.org/blog/2022/04/simple-chains)). Every PINN derivative benchmark located runs on a GPU (RTX 6000, A100, A6000, RTX 5080, T600); no CPU PINN step time was found.

The consolidated verdict for a compiled per-equation Taylor-mode CPU engine follows. The absences rest on about 30 searches for the engine itself; the notes did not inspect how `jax.experimental.jet` implements tanh or the internals of commercial codes.

## Dairy plants already run grey-box MPC; adoption turns on lifecycle, not accuracy

What runs on real dairy plants is classical. Petersen's industrial PhD with GEA (DTU, 2016) put MPC with real-time optimization on a four-stage spray dryer producing about 7,500 kg/h of milk powder, using first-principles grey-box models: against PI control it raised profit by **about 228,000 € per year, product rate by 322 kg/h, moved residual moisture 0.166 percentage points closer to the limit and improved energy efficiency by 1 %**, and was "fully integrated in the daily operation" ([DTU Orbit](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/economic-model-predictive-control-for-spray-drying-plants/)). Vendor case studies report similar margins: Rockwell's Pavilion8 at Miraka claims more than 4 % capacity and above 1 % energy saving per tonne ([Rockwell](https://www.rockwellautomation.com/en-au/company/news/case-studies/miraka-benefits-from-model-predictive-control-solution.html)), and GEA's OptiPartner claims up to 10 % capacity ([GEA](https://www.gea.com/en/stories/optimization-in-spray-drying-starts-with-understanding/)); both are vendor claims. Hybrid models validated on real evaporator data appeared in 2026 from DTU and GEA: on a four-effect falling-film evaporator with industrial data, a mechanistic fouling-layer model "substantially reduces prediction error" while the empirical heat-transfer-loss model "lacks predictive capability and therefore cannot be applied for model-based prediction or control" (ESCAPE 36 proceedings; no numeric error on the abstract page) ([LAPSE](https://psecommunity.org/LAPSE:2026.0368v1)). That is direct published support for physics-structured fouling estimation.

The notes found **no PINN validated on a spray dryer or falling-film evaporator with plant data**. The nearest PINN dryer twin is for a surgical-instrument dryer (IEEE SIMPAR 2025) ([SDU](https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/en/publications/an-open-sourced-digital-twin-model-of-an-industrial-dryer/)), and process-engineering PINNs are still tested mostly on simulators, including a 2026 distillation twin trained on 961 synthetic measurements ([arXiv 2603.24644](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24644v1)). A simulated-plant PINN result is therefore level with the academic state of the art and behind the industrial one. Benefits in this sector are quoted in small percentages, so a twin's uncertainty must be tighter than about 1 % energy or 0.2 points of moisture to matter commercially. The groups to position against or approach are DTU with GEA, and TUHH with Nestlé on hybrid spray-drying models ([TUHH](https://www.tuhh.de/spe/research/current-research-projects/hybrid-modeling-of-spray-drying-processes)). No publication from Fonterra, Arla, FrieslandCampina or Tetra Pak on hybrid or PINN twins was found, and journal-level searches of Drying Technology and the Journal of Food Engineering were not possible.

The requirements literature is consistent that the bottleneck is deployment. Chew et al. (AIChE Journal 72, 2026) write that "AI models must be treated like any other engineered system, with validation, monitoring, and governance", managed through "commissioning, monitoring, drift detection, re-qualification, and redeployment, with documented tests and sign-offs"; they prescribe shadow-mode commissioning validated on historian data with a published model card, then deployment behind safety layers, with retraining triggered by drift, management-of-change events or equipment change, and they recommend grey-box modelling where feasible ([Chalmers](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/551339/file/551339_Fulltext.pdf)). Japan's control survey, as summarized later, found the most pressing problem with inferential sensors is "not model accuracy, but rather, model maintenance" ([arXiv 2211.06440](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.06440)). Integration runs through ISA-95 layers and OPC UA, with NAMUR Open Architecture (NE 175) providing a read-only second channel beside the control system ([OPC Foundation](https://opcfoundation.org/?p=5450)). For regulated products such as infant formula and pharmaceutical spray drying, ICH Q13 has a process-models section ([EMA](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/scientific-guideline/ich-guideline-q13-continuous-manufacturing-drug-substances-drug-products-step-5_en.pdf)), and the EU GMP draft Annex 22 on AI, per a law-firm summary the notes did not check against the draft, covers only models with deterministic output for identical inputs ([Mondaq](https://webiis10.mondaq.com/new-technology/1650932/eu-consults-on-new-gmp-rules-for-ai-in-pharma-manufacturing)). Estimating physical parameters of a fixed model is easier to defend under that direction than retraining network weights in production. Adoption surveys cited by Chew et al. report that 72 % of organizations with pre-2019 AI pilots had deployed nothing to production.

The path from a simulated plant to industrial readiness is therefore offline validation on historian data from a real unit across operating regimes and a full fouling-and-cleaning cycle, then shadow mode with a model card, calibrated uncertainty with input-validity checks, a maintenance protocol with drift detection tied to change control, OPC UA read access on an advisory basis before any closed loop behind the existing safety and MPC layers, and a benchmark against the incumbent grey-box model with a Kalman filter or MPC inferential. The peer-reviewed record gives no reason to expect a PINN to beat a mechanistic model with a moving-horizon or Kalman estimator where the forward solve is cheap; the defensible niche is an expensive forward model or a twin amortized across conditions. The notes found no post-mortem of a failed PINN deployment in a process plant, which reflects how few have been attempted, and did not research historian integration specifics, functional-safety certification or food-specific regulation.

## Conclusion

The six bets are less independent than they look. Hard constraints are what make an exact solve reach machine precision; an exact last layer plus second-order updates of the hidden weights is the published cure for random-feature scale sensitivity; curvature removes most of what loss-weight controllers exist to fix; and a cached trunk with a linear head is the same linear algebra as an extreme learning machine, reused online. One stack follows from that: a hard ansatz, a projected linear layer, Gauss-Newton on what remains, and closed-form derivatives everywhere. Each pairwise piece is published, the full stack is not, and every published timing for its parts is dominated by Python, MATLAB or generic AD overhead. The engine's defensible contribution is the reverse pass generated per equation and the measurements it enables; Cao et al. set the line to draw against, and a further sweep of late-2026 preprints is prudent before any novelty claim is written, given how many of the closest results appeared between May and September 2026.

The second implication is that negative results are assets here. The field's documented weakness is weak baselines and unreported failures, so a benchmark that shows the classical solver's curve, a controller study that ends in a null result, and a twin that loses to classical estimators on a cheap-forward-solve unit all sit in the under-published part of the literature. Industrial readiness is not reachable through any of the six bets alone: it requires plant data, a maintenance protocol and a comparison with the grey-box MPC that has run in dairy plants since 2016.
